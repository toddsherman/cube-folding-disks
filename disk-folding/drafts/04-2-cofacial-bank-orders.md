## 4.2. Cofacial bank orders by straight cuts

We order the cells of one all-flip region by induction along straight runs of its joins. As in Section 2, these cuts are decompositions for the proof: every retained join of the region is kept and checked. The external inputs are standard PL topology: the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem and the \(\theta\)-graph lemma, which give (Sch) below, the Schoenflies form of the fact used in Lemma R1; and the classification of compact PL surfaces, which also underlies the recognition criterion of Section 1.1.

**(Sch)** Let \(\Gamma\) be a proper PL arc with endpoints \(p\ne q\) in a closed PL disk \(D\). Then \(p,q\) divide \(\partial D\) into two arcs \(\gamma_1,\gamma_2\), the complement \(D\setminus \Gamma\) has exactly two components, and their closures \(D_1,D_2\) are closed disks with \(\partial D_i=\gamma_i\cup \Gamma\), \(D_1\cup D_2=D\) and \(D_1\cap D_2=\Gamma\).

Identify \(D\) PL with a convex polygon; the interior of \(\Gamma\) lies in the interior of \(D\). The \(\theta\)-graph \(\gamma_1\cup\gamma_2\cup\Gamma\) divides the plane into exactly three faces, each bounded by one of its three cycles [MT01, Section 2.1]. The unbounded face contains \(\mathbb R^2\setminus D\), whose closure contains \(\partial D\), so it is the face bounded by \(\gamma_1\cup\gamma_2=\partial D\). The other two faces \(U_1,U_2\), bounded by \(\gamma_1\cup\Gamma\) and \(\gamma_2\cup\Gamma\), are therefore the components of \(\operatorname{int}D\setminus\Gamma\). By the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem, \(D_i=U_i\cup\gamma_i\cup\Gamma\) is a closed disk with \(\partial D_i=\gamma_i\cup\Gamma\). Then \(D_1\cup D_2=D\), and \(D_1\cap D_2=\Gamma\) because \(U_1\cap U_2=\emptyset\) and \(\gamma_1\cap\gamma_2=\{p,q\}\). The sets \(D_i\setminus\Gamma=U_i\cup(\gamma_i\setminus\{p,q\})\) are connected, disjoint and relatively closed in \(D\setminus\Gamma\), so they are its two components.

Throughout, \(Q\) is an **all-flip physical disk**: a physical source in the sense of Section 1.1 that is homeomorphic to a closed disk, with all cells on one face. Such a source is a compact PL surface homeomorphic to a disk, so by the classification of compact PL surfaces it is a PL disk, and (Sch) applies to it. These are the regions supplied by (G) for whichever member of the toggle family is in use, equivalently the contracted monochrome disk regions of Section 2, including those left by the leaf reflections of L6: a contraction, or a leaf reflection in L6, glues two disks along an arc that lies in the boundary of each (the whole interface run), and the result is a disk. Distinct cells are distinct lattice squares, and corners are identified only through retained joins. Hence a connected physical source has a connected join graph, and the coordinate map \(\pi:Q\to\mathbb R^2\) is injective on every straight run of joins. A vertex occurrence (corner fan) is *interior*, or a *full star*, when its fan has four cells and all four joins (a circular link, as in Section 1.1; the full retained star of Lemma R1), and *boundary* otherwise; a boundary occurrence is *joint* when its fan has at least two cells. The counterclockwise boundary walk \(W=(W_0,\ldots,W_{L-1})\), with \(Q\) on the left, lists the boundary halfedges. A *position* \(t\in\mathbb Z/L\) is the boundary occurrence between \(W_{t-1}\) and \(W_t\), and these are its two *walk halfedges*. Boundary fans are intervals, so each boundary occurrence is exactly one position. Banks are intervals of \(W\), as in Section 4.1.

**Colors.** Fix a root cell \(c_0=(x_0,y_0)\) of \(Q\), with frame \(\beta_{c_0}(x,y)=o+xu+yv\), and let \(\tau:\mathbb R\to[0,1]\) be the distance to the nearest even integer. For a cell \(c=(x_c,y_c)\), the frames
\[
\beta_c(x,y)=o+\tau(x_c+x-x_0)\,u+\tau(y_c+y-y_0)\,v,\qquad 0\le x,y\le1,
\]
satisfy the join equations of Section 1.1 at every flip. Across a vertical side the two frames agree on the side and are mirror images in it, because \(\tau\) is even about every integer. So crossing the side reverses the target coordinate along \(u\) and fixes the one along \(v\); likewise across horizontal sides. A parent frame and a flip determine the child frame (proof of Lemma R2), and \(Q\) is join-connected, so these are the frames of \(Q\). Hence every side on a vertical lattice line \(X=\xi\), boundary sides included, maps onto the target edge on which the \(u\)-coordinate equals \(\tau(\xi-x_0)\in\{0,1\}\). This edge depends only on the parity of \(\xi\), and the two edges so obtained are opposite; call them the *vertical* target edges, and the two opposite edges met in the same way by horizontal lines the *horizontal* ones. Fix one of the two partitions \(\Pi\) of the target edges into adjacent pairs, and call its two classes, the *merged colors*, *red* and *blue*. Each class contains one vertical and one horizontal edge, so the colors of vertical lines alternate with parity, and so do those of horizontal lines. A join, side or port is colored by its target edge. A lattice point, and any position over it, is **pure red** (**pure blue**) when both lattice lines through it are red (blue), and **mixed** otherwise.

Two cyclically consecutive selected banks *meet* at a position \(t\) when the last halfedge of the first is \(W_{t-1}\) and the first halfedge of the second is \(W_t\).

**Lemma L2 (cofacial bank orders).** Let \(Q\) be an all-flip physical disk, and select banks on its boundary. Suppose that for one adjacent-pair partition \(\Pi\):

(H1) in the cyclic sequence of selected halfedges, listed in the order of \(W\), each merged color is consecutive (equivalently, the color changes at most twice);

(H2) both merged colors are selected, or some boundary halfedge is unselected, or two cyclically consecutive selected banks meet at a mixed position.

Then \(Q\) has a locally feasible order that exposes every selected halfedge, is monotone on every selected bank, and gives any two selected banks on the same target edge separated parent sets. No outwardness is assumed.

*Remarks.* (i) A walk traverses a bank in one tangent direction, and its consecutive parents are joined along the tangent. Monotonicity along \(W\) is therefore the tangent-coordinate monotonicity of Section 4.1. (ii) Condition (H2) fails exactly when every boundary halfedge is selected, all in one merged color, and every position at which two consecutive banks meet is pure. (H2) is sufficient; we do not claim that it is necessary. (iii) If the hypotheses hold for one partition, they hold for every partition satisfying (H1). Indeed, suppose \(\Pi\) satisfies (H1) but not (H2). Then every boundary halfedge is selected and lies on one of the two edges \(e_V,e_H\) of a single \(\Pi\)-class. Under the other partition, the color of a boundary halfedge is just its direction. Consecutive horizontal halfedges of \(W\) are collinear, and the north side of a topmost cell and the south side of a bottommost cell are on different horizontal lines. So the horizontal halfedges form at least two maximal runs along \(W\), the direction changes at least four times, and the other partition fails (H1). So a partition satisfying (H1) but not (H2) is the only partition satisfying (H1).

The proof occupies the rest of this subsection.

**Lemma L2.1 (parity).**

1. Every unit lattice segment has exactly one pure endpoint, and that endpoint has the segment's color.
2. A cell has one pure red and one pure blue corner, which are diagonally opposite, and two mixed corners. Its two sides at a pure corner have that corner's color. Its two sides at a mixed corner have different colors.
3. At a pure position, both walk halfedges have its color. At a non-joint position of a cell \(c\), the two walk halfedges are the two sides of \(c\) there.
4. Pure and mixed positions alternate along \(W\).
5. A cell has exactly one side on each target edge. Hence selected banks on one target edge have disjoint parent sets.

**Proof.** A segment on a line of color \(\lambda\) joins points on two consecutive perpendicular lines, one of each color. Its endpoint on the \(\lambda\)-colored line is pure of color \(\lambda\), and the other endpoint is mixed. This is 1. The corners of a cell lie on two consecutive vertical and two consecutive horizontal lines, so 1 gives 2. For 3, a halfedge at a pure point has that point as its pure endpoint; the second claim is the definition of a one-cell fan. Consecutive positions are the endpoints of one halfedge, so 1 gives 4. The two vertical sides of a cell lie on lines of different parity, and so do its two horizontal sides. Banks select disjoint halfedges, which gives 5. ∎

A **cut** of \(Q\) is a maximal straight run of retained joins along one lattice line, all of whose interior vertices are interior occurrences. This is the continuation rule of Lemma R1, applied here to every join of an all-flip region.

**Lemma L2.2 (cuts split the disk).** Let \(\Gamma\) be a cut of \(Q\).

1. Every join lies on exactly one cut. The endpoints of \(\Gamma\) are distinct positions. All joins of \(\Gamma\) have one target edge and hence one color, and a pure endpoint of \(\Gamma\) has that color.
2. \(\Gamma\) divides \(Q\) into two all-flip physical disks \(Q_1,Q_2\), its *halves*, with \(Q_1\cup Q_2=Q\) and \(Q_1\cap Q_2=\Gamma\). The two cells of each join of \(\Gamma\) lie in different \(Q_i\). Every other join has both cells in one \(Q_i\), and the joins of \(Q_i\) are exactly the joins of \(Q\) between its cells. In particular \(1\le|Q_i|<|Q|\).
3. The halfedges of \(W\) with parent in \(Q_i\) form a nonempty interval \(\alpha_i\) of \(W\), and \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\) are complementary. Name the endpoints of \(\Gamma\) so that \(W\) passes from \(\alpha_1\) to \(\alpha_2\) at \(p\) and from \(\alpha_2\) to \(\alpha_1\) at \(q\). The walk of \(Q_1\) is \(\alpha_1\), from \(q\) to \(p\), followed by the block \(\kappa_1\) of cut sides of its cells, from \(p\) to \(q\). The walk of \(Q_2\) is \(\alpha_2\), from \(p\) to \(q\), followed by \(\kappa_2\), from \(q\) to \(p\).
4. The parents of \(\kappa_1\), from \(p\) to \(q\), are distinct cells \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), each \(a_i\) joined across \(\Gamma\) to \(b_i\in Q_2\). The parents of \(\kappa_2\), from \(q\) to \(p\), are \(b_k,\ldots,b_1\).

**Proof.** *Part 1.* Extend a join along its line in both directions. At an interior occurrence the full star contains the next collinear join; at a boundary occurrence the run stops. Since \(\pi\) is injective on the run, it cannot close up, and its two endpoints have different coordinates. A lattice line carries joins of only one target edge. A pure endpoint is the pure endpoint of the last join of \(\Gamma\), so it has that join's color by L2.1.

*Part 2.* The open sides of the joins of \(\Gamma\) and its interior vertices are interior points of \(Q\), while its endpoints lie on \(\partial Q\). So \(\Gamma\) is a proper arc. Apply (Sch) to obtain closed disks \(D_1,D_2\). An open cell misses \(\Gamma\), so each closed cell lies in one \(D_i\). Near a point of an open side not on \(\Gamma\), the source is a union of two half-disks, one in each adjacent cell, missing \(\Gamma\); so both cells of a join not on \(\Gamma\) lie in one \(D_i\). Near a point \(x\) of an open side on \(\Gamma\), the two half-disks are the two components of a neighborhood minus \(\Gamma\). Since \(x\in D_1\cap D_2\), both components of \(Q\setminus \Gamma\) meet this neighborhood, so the two cells lie in different \(D_i\).

Let \(Q_i\) be the physical source formed by the cells in \(D_i\) and the joins of \(Q\) among them; joins between two cells of \(D_i\) are not on \(\Gamma\). A fan of \(Q\) at a vertex off \(\Gamma\) contains no join of \(\Gamma\) and lies in one \(D_i\). At a vertex of \(\Gamma\) the fan contains one join of \(\Gamma\) (at an endpoint) or two opposite ones (at an interior vertex); removing them from the path or four-cycle leaves two subpaths, one in each \(D_i\). Since corners are identified only through joins, \(Q_i\) makes exactly the identifications that \(Q\) makes among points of \(D_i\). Every point of \(D_i\) lies in a closed cell of \(D_i\): a point of \(D_1\) in no \(D_1\)-cell would lie in a \(D_2\)-cell, hence in \(D_1\cap D_2=\Gamma\), which is covered by the cut sides of \(D_1\)-cells; likewise for \(D_2\). The induced map \(Q_i\to D_i\) is therefore a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space, hence a homeomorphism. So \(Q_i\) is an all-flip physical disk; it is connected, so its join graph is connected. It contains one cell of each join of \(\Gamma\), so \(1\le|Q_i|<|Q|\).

*Part 3.* By (Sch), \(D_1\cap\partial Q=\gamma_1\) is one of the two boundary arcs between \(p\) and \(q\). A halfedge with parent in \(Q_1\) lies in this arc, and conversely. The counterclockwise walk of \(Q_1\) traverses \(\alpha_1\) with the same cells on the left as \(W\), hence in the same direction, and then traverses \(\Gamma\). The same holds for \(Q_2\).

*Part 4.* Interior vertices of \(\Gamma\) are full stars, so consecutive \(a_i\) are joined, and \(\pi\) is injective on \(\Gamma\). ∎

**Lemma L2.3 (cuts separate cells).** Any two distinct cells \(a,b\) of \(Q\) lie in different halves of some cut of \(Q\).

**Proof.** Induct on \(|Q|\). Since \(Q\) is join-connected, it has a join, and that join lies on a cut \(\Gamma\). If \(\Gamma\) separates \(a\) and \(b\) we are done. Otherwise both lie in one half, which we name \(Q_1\), and \(|Q|\ge3\). By induction, a cut \(\Gamma'\) of \(Q_1\) has \(a,b\) in different halves \(Q_a\ni a\) and \(Q_b\ni b\) of \(Q_1\), with \(Q_a\cap Q_b=\Gamma'\).

The joins of \(\Gamma'\) are joins of \(Q\) not on \(\Gamma\), and full stars of \(Q_1\) are full stars of \(Q\). Interior points of \(\Gamma'\) are interior points of \(Q_1\), while \(\Gamma\subset\partial Q_1\). So \(\Gamma'\) meets \(\Gamma\) only in endpoints of \(\Gamma'\). Let \(\Gamma''\) be the cut of \(Q\) containing \(\Gamma'\). It extends \(\Gamma'\) beyond an endpoint \(z\) exactly when \(z\) is an interior occurrence of \(Q\). Since \(z\in\partial Q_1=\gamma_1\cup \Gamma\), this happens only when \(z\) is an interior vertex of \(\Gamma\). In that case the star at \(z\) has two \(Q_1\)-cells in one half-plane of the lattice line containing \(\pi(\Gamma)\) and two \(Q_2\)-cells in the other, and the only join of \(Q_1\) at \(z\) is the one between its two cells there. So \(\Gamma'\) reaches \(z\) perpendicular to that line. Since \(\pi(\Gamma')\) is a straight segment, it meets that line in at most one point, and \(\pi\) is injective on \(\Gamma'\). So in that case \(\Gamma'\cap\Gamma=\{z\}\), and in particular at most one endpoint of \(\Gamma'\) is an interior vertex of \(\Gamma\).

*Case 1: \(\Gamma''=\Gamma'\).* Then \(\Gamma'\cap \Gamma\subset\{p,q\}\). The connected set \(\Gamma\setminus\{p,q\}\) lies in \(Q_1\setminus \Gamma'=(Q_a\setminus \Gamma')\sqcup(Q_b\setminus \Gamma')\), a disjoint union of relatively open sets. Hence it lies in one of them, say \(Q_a\setminus \Gamma'\), after exchanging the names \(a,b\) if necessary. A \(Q_1\)-cell along \(\Gamma\) has its open cut side in \(Q_a\setminus \Gamma'\), so it lies in \(Q_a\); otherwise its closed square would lie in \(Q_b\). Therefore every join of \(Q\) not on \(\Gamma''\) joins \(Q_a\) to \(Q_a\), \(Q_b\) to \(Q_b\), \(Q_2\) to \(Q_2\), or \(Q_a\) to \(Q_2\) (the joins of \(\Gamma\)). By L2.2 for \(\Gamma''\), each half of \(\Gamma''\) is a disk, hence join-connected, and only joins of \(\Gamma''\) connect the two halves. So the halves of \(\Gamma''\) are the join-components of \(Q\) after removing the joins of \(\Gamma''\). The set \(Q_b\) is closed under the remaining joins and misses \(a\). Hence \(a\) and \(b\) lie in different halves of \(\Gamma''\).

*Case 2: \(\Gamma'\) ends at an interior vertex \(z\) of \(\Gamma\).* Then \(\Gamma'\cap \Gamma=\{z\}\), as just shown, and \(\Gamma''=\Gamma'\cup \Gamma_2'\). Here \(\Gamma_2'\) continues straight from \(z\), through the join between the two \(Q_2\)-cells at \(z\), to an endpoint \(z_2\in\partial Q\).

The image \(\pi(\Gamma_2')\) meets the lattice line of \(\pi(\Gamma)\) only in \(\pi(z)\), and \(\pi\) is injective on \(\Gamma''\). So the connected set \(\Gamma_2'\setminus\{z\}\) misses \(\Gamma\). It contains the open side of the join between the two \(Q_2\)-cells at \(z\), a subset of \(Q_2\setminus \Gamma\), so it lies in \(Q_2\setminus \Gamma\). Therefore \(\Gamma_2'\) consists of joins of \(Q_2\), its interior vertices are full stars of \(Q_2\), and its endpoints \(z,z_2\) lie on \(\partial Q_2\): it is a cut of \(Q_2\).

The two \(Q_1\)-cells at \(z\) are the cells of the last join of \(\Gamma'\), so one lies in \(Q_a\) and the other in \(Q_b\). The point \(z\) divides \(\Gamma\) into subarcs \(\Gamma_a,\Gamma_b\), named so that the \(Q_a\)-cell at \(z\) has its cut side on \(\Gamma_a\) and the \(Q_b\)-cell on \(\Gamma_b\). The two \(Q_2\)-cells at \(z\) are the cells of the first join of \(\Gamma_2'\), so they lie in different halves of \(\Gamma_2'\). Let \(Q_{2,a}\) be the half containing the one with its cut side on \(\Gamma_a\), and \(Q_{2,b}\) the other half. The set \(\Gamma_a\setminus\{z\}\) is connected, misses \(\Gamma'\) and \(\Gamma_2'\), and contains the open cut sides of these two cells. As in Case 1, every \(Q_1\)-cell along \(\Gamma_a\) lies in \(Q_a\) and every \(Q_2\)-cell along \(\Gamma_a\) lies in \(Q_{2,a}\). Likewise the cells along \(\Gamma_b\) lie in \(Q_b\) and \(Q_{2,b}\).

Every join of \(Q\) outside \(\Gamma''\) therefore lies within \(Q_a\), \(Q_b\), \(Q_{2,a}\) or \(Q_{2,b}\), or joins \(Q_a\) to \(Q_{2,a}\) or \(Q_b\) to \(Q_{2,b}\). The set \(Q_b\cup Q_{2,b}\) is closed under these joins and misses \(a\). As in Case 1, \(a\) and \(b\) lie in different halves of \(\Gamma''\). ∎

**Marked selections.** In Lemma L2.4 a *selection* \(\Sigma\) is an arbitrary set of boundary halfedges, and *markers* are two positions \(t_R,t_B\), with a *tie flag* when they coincide. The *red interval* \(I_R\) consists of the halfedges met walking forward from \(t_R\) to \(t_B\), and the *blue interval* \(I_B\) of the rest, from \(t_B\) to \(t_R\). For coincident markers the flag is their order: "\(t_R\) first" means that \(t_R\) precedes \(t_B\), so that \(I_R=\emptyset\), and "\(t_B\) first" means that \(t_B\) precedes \(t_R\), so that \(I_B=\emptyset\). The *color condition* requires every halfedge of \(\Sigma\cap I_R\) to be red and every halfedge of \(\Sigma\cap I_B\) to be blue. The *stretch* of a position is the maximal run of consecutive positions containing it that crosses no halfedge of \(\Sigma\), or all of \(W\) if \(\Sigma=\emptyset\). A marker is *admissible* when its stretch contains a mixed position. A *slide* moves a marker within its stretch without passing the other marker; it may land on the other marker, the flag then recording their previous order. A slide may separate coincident markers only in a way that keeps their order: with \(t_R\) first, only \(t_R\) moves backward or \(t_B\) forward; with \(t_B\) first, the reverse. Markers in different stretches impose no order constraint on each other. A slide does not change the sequences \(\Sigma\cap I_R\) and \(\Sigma\cap I_B\), so it preserves the color condition, admissibility and conclusion (S3) below. If \(\Sigma=\emptyset\), every placement of the markers satisfies the color condition and (S3) is vacuous; a slide is then any move, and the requirements below on the order of markers are void.

**Lemma L2.4 (marked orders).** Let \(\Sigma\) be a selection whose markers are admissible and satisfy the color condition. Then \(Q\) has a strict total order with the following properties.

(S1) The red joins are pairwise nonalternating, and so are the blue joins.

(S2) The parent of a red (blue) halfedge of \(\Sigma\) is not strictly between the endpoints of any red (blue) join.

(S3) Along \(I_R\), the parents of \(\Sigma\cap I_R\) are weakly increasing. Along \(I_B\), the parents of \(\Sigma\cap I_B\) are weakly decreasing.

**Proof.** Induct on \(|Q|\). A single cell has no joins and one parent. Let \(|Q|\ge2\). For a cut with halves and endpoints named as in L2.2, write \(\bar\alpha_1\) for the positions from \(q\) to \(p\) along \(\alpha_1\) and \(\bar\alpha_2\) for those from \(p\) to \(q\), with both ends included.

*Step 1: a separating cut.* We claim that, after slides, both markers are mixed and some cut \(\Gamma\) has \(t_R\in\bar\alpha_1\) and \(t_B\in\bar\alpha_2\). Moreover, coincident markers then lie at \(p\) with \(t_R\) first, or at \(q\) with \(t_B\) first.

The last requirement is a matter of naming. At an endpoint \(z\) of \(\Gamma\), the walk leaves one half's interval and enters the other's. Call the half left \(Q_1\) when \(t_R\) is first, so that \(z=p\), and the half entered \(Q_1\) when \(t_B\) is first, so that \(z=q\). Positions other than \(p,q\) lie in only one of \(\bar\alpha_1,\bar\alpha_2\).

Slide each marker to a mixed position of its stretch, keeping the order of markers that share a stretch.

*Case 1: the stretch of some marker contains a mixed joint position \(w\).* Slide the markers of that stretch to \(w\): first the one nearer to \(w\) along the stretch (for coincident markers, the one that may leave the tie toward \(w\)), then the other if it shares the stretch, which lands on the first with the flag recording their order. Two cells of the fan at \(w\) are joined along a side ending at \(w\), and the cut through this join ends at the boundary position \(w\). A cut endpoint lies in both \(\bar\alpha_i\), so the halves can be named as required.

*Case 2: neither stretch contains a mixed joint position.* Then each *available* mixed position \(x\), that is, each mixed position in the stretch of a marker, is a one-cell fan of a cell \(c(x)\), and both walk halfedges at \(x\) lie on \(c(x)\) (L2.1). Suppose the markers can be placed by slides at mixed positions \(x_R,x_B\) with \(c(x_R)\ne c(x_B)\). Then L2.3 gives a cut separating these cells. Naming the half of \(c(x_R)\) as \(Q_1\), we get \(t_R=x_R\) interior to \(\bar\alpha_1\) and \(t_B=x_B\) interior to \(\bar\alpha_2\).

Otherwise all available mixed positions are corners of one cell \(c\). Two distinct ones would be the two mixed corners of \(c\), both non-joint, and all four sides of \(c\) would be unjoined, which is impossible for \(|Q|\ge2\). So both markers lie in the stretch \(\Lambda\) of a single mixed position \(w\), and \(w\) is the only mixed position of \(\Lambda\).

- If \(\Sigma=\emptyset\), then \(\Lambda=W\), and every halfedge has \(w\) as its mixed endpoint. So \(W\) consists of the two walk halfedges at \(w\). These are perpendicular sides of \(c\) and do not close up, a contradiction.
- If \(\Sigma\ne\emptyset\), let \(\omega_-,\omega_+\) be the selected halfedges immediately before and after \(\Lambda\). By L2.1, \(\Lambda\) consists of \(w\) and possibly a pure neighbor at either end. If \(\Lambda\) begins at \(w\), then \(\omega_-=W_{w-1}\). If \(\Lambda\) begins at a pure position \(y\), then \(\omega_-\) and \(W_{w-1}\) are both walk halfedges at \(y\) and have its color. So \(\omega_-\) has the color of \(W_{w-1}\), and likewise \(\omega_+\) has the color of \(W_w\). These are the two sides of \(c\) at \(w\), which have different colors. So \(\Sigma\) contains both colors. But the markers coincide in the \(\Sigma\)-free stretch \(\Lambda\), so one of \(I_R,I_B\) contains all of \(\Sigma\), and the color condition makes \(\Sigma\) one color. This is a contradiction.

*Step 2: intervals.* For \(t\in\bar\alpha_1\), let \(\alpha_1^{<t}\) and \(\alpha_1^{>t}\) be the halfedges of \(\alpha_1\) from \(q\) to \(t\) and from \(t\) to \(p\). For \(t\in\bar\alpha_2\), let \(\alpha_2^{<t}\) and \(\alpha_2^{>t}\) be those of \(\alpha_2\) from \(p\) to \(t\) and from \(t\) to \(q\). Then
\[
I_R=\alpha_1^{>t_R}\,\alpha_2^{<t_B},\qquad I_B=\alpha_2^{>t_B}\,\alpha_1^{<t_R}.
\]
Walking forward from \(t_R\), the walk traverses \(\alpha_1^{>t_R}\) to \(p\), meeting no position of \(\bar\alpha_2\) other than \(p\) and its starting point (when \(t_R=q\)), and then traverses \(\alpha_2\) up to \(t_B\). The two ties are read off directly. With both markers at \(p\) and \(t_R\) first, \(I_R=\emptyset=\alpha_1^{>p}\alpha_2^{<p}\). With both at \(q\) and \(t_B\) first, \(I_R=W=\alpha_1^{>q}\alpha_2^{<q}\). In every case \(I_B\) is the complement of \(I_R\).

*Step 3: subproblems.* Put \(\Sigma_i=(\Sigma\cap\alpha_i)\cup\kappa_i\). In the walk of \(Q_1\), let \(p_1\) be the position between \(\alpha_1\) and \(\kappa_1\), and \(q_1\) the position between \(\kappa_1\) and \(\alpha_1\). In the walk of \(Q_2\), let \(q_2\) be the position between \(\alpha_2\) and \(\kappa_2\), and \(p_2\) the position between \(\kappa_2\) and \(\alpha_2\). The subproblem \(Q_1\) inherits \(t_R\), and \(Q_2\) inherits \(t_B\); write \(t_R^1=t_R\) and \(t_B^2=t_B\) for the inherited markers, so that subproblem \(i\) has markers \(t_R^i,t_B^i\). A marker at \(p\) or \(q\) becomes \(p_i\) or \(q_i\), and other positions are unchanged. The new markers are \(t_B^1=q_1\) and \(t_R^2=q_2\), both with "\(t_B\) first", for a red cut, and \(t_B^1=p_1\) and \(t_R^2=p_2\), both with "\(t_R\) first", for a blue cut.

Reading the walks \(\alpha_i\kappa_i\) gives, for every position of the inherited markers:
\[
\text{red cut: } I^1_R=\alpha_1^{>t_R}\kappa_1,\quad I^1_B=\alpha_1^{<t_R},\quad I^2_R=\kappa_2\alpha_2^{<t_B},\quad I^2_B=\alpha_2^{>t_B};
\]
\[
\text{blue cut: } I^1_R=\alpha_1^{>t_R},\quad I^1_B=\kappa_1\alpha_1^{<t_R},\quad I^2_R=\alpha_2^{<t_B},\quad I^2_B=\alpha_2^{>t_B}\kappa_2.
\]
Before the slides below, coincident markers occur in a subproblem exactly when the cut is red and \(t_R=q\) or \(t_B=q\), or the cut is blue and \(t_R=p\) or \(t_B=p\). The flags were chosen to give these formulas in those cases. With Step 2, the formulas give
\[
I_R=(I^1_R\setminus\kappa_1)(I^2_R\setminus\kappa_2),\qquad I_B=(I^2_B\setminus\kappa_2)(I^1_B\setminus\kappa_1).
\]

New markers at a pure position are moved. For a red cut with \(q\) pure, move \(t_B^1\) forward from \(q_1\) over the first halfedge \(h^+\) of \(\alpha_1\), and \(t_R^2\) backward from \(q_2\) over the last halfedge \(h^-\) of \(\alpha_2\). For a blue cut with \(p\) pure, move \(t_B^1\) backward from \(p_1\) over the last halfedge \(g^-\) of \(\alpha_1\), and \(t_R^2\) forward from \(p_2\) over the first halfedge \(g^+\) of \(\alpha_2\).

These moves are slides. Take a red cut with \(q\) pure. Then \(q\) is pure red (L2.2), so \(h^\pm\) are red (L2.1). These halfedges lie in \(\alpha_1\) or \(\alpha_2\), so they are in \(\Sigma_i\) only if they are in \(\Sigma\). If \(h^+\in\Sigma\), the color condition puts it in \(I_R\cap\alpha_1=\alpha_1^{>t_R}\). Since \(h^+\) is the first halfedge of \(\alpha_1\), this forces \(t_R=q\). If \(h^-\in\Sigma\), then \(h^-\in I_R\cap\alpha_2=\alpha_2^{<t_B}\), forcing \(t_B=q\). For a blue cut with \(p\) pure, \(g^-\in\Sigma\) would give \(g^-\in\alpha_1^{<t_R}\) and force \(t_R=p\), and \(g^+\in\Sigma\) would give \(g^+\in\alpha_2^{>t_B}\) and force \(t_B=p\). In each case the forced marker sits at a pure position, contrary to Step 1; so none of \(h^\pm,g^\pm\) is selected.

Each move takes one step onto a mixed position (L2.1). The other marker of the subproblem is inherited and hence mixed, so the move passes nothing. If the moved marker lands on the other one, the order before the move is the assigned flag: \(t_B^i\) precedes \(t_R^i\) for a red cut, and \(t_R^i\) precedes \(t_B^i\) for a blue cut. These landings are the only other coincidences, and their flags are again "\(t_B\) first" for a red cut and "\(t_R\) first" for a blue cut. So the selected parts of the four intervals are still those of the displayed formulas. All four markers are now mixed and hence admissible. The color condition holds in both subproblems: for a red cut, \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_1^{>t_R}\subset\Sigma\cap I_R\) and \(\kappa_1\) are red and \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_1^{<t_R}\subset\Sigma\cap I_B\) is blue, and \(Q_2\) is the same; a blue cut is symmetric, with \(\kappa_i\) blue. By induction, \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) have orders \(<_1\) and \(<_2\) satisfying (S1)–(S3).

*Step 4: combination.* Let \(O\) list \(<_1\) followed by \(<_2\). Use the notation of L2.2.

*Red cut.*

- *Order of the cut parents.* In \(Q_1\), \(\kappa_1\) is the final part of \(\Sigma_1\cap I_R^1\) in walk order, so (S3) and distinctness give \(a_1<\cdots<a_k\). A red halfedge of \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_1\) lies in \(\alpha_1^{>t_R}\), before \(\kappa_1\), so its parent \(\mu\) satisfies \(\mu\le a_1\). In \(Q_2\), \(\kappa_2\) is the initial part of \(\Sigma_2\cap I_R^2\). So \(b_k<\cdots<b_1\), and every red parent \(\mu\) of \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_2\) satisfies \(\mu\ge b_1\).
- *(S1).* Joins inside one block are handled by induction, and joins in different blocks occupy disjoint intervals of \(O\). Since \(a_1<\cdots<a_k<b_k<\cdots<b_1\), the cut joins are nested. A red join \(xy\) of \(Q_1\) alternates with \(a_ib_i\) only if \(x<a_i<y\). This is excluded by (S2) in \(Q_1\), because the cut side of \(a_i\) is a red halfedge of \(\Sigma_1\). The case of \(Q_2\) is symmetric. No blue join meets both blocks.
- *(S2).* Against joins of its own block, a parent is protected by induction. Against joins of the other block, it is protected by disjointness of the blocks. Against the cut joins, only red parents need checking. A red parent \(\mu\in Q_1\) is strictly inside \(a_ib_i\) only if \(a_i<\mu\), which contradicts \(\mu\le a_1\). A red parent \(\mu\in Q_2\) is strictly inside only if \(\mu<b_i\), which contradicts \(\mu\ge b_1\).

*Blue cut.* Now \(\kappa_1\) is the initial part of \(\Sigma_1\cap I_B^1\) and \(\kappa_2\) the final part of \(\Sigma_2\cap I_B^2\). This gives \(a_k<\cdots<a_1<b_1<\cdots<b_k\). Every blue parent \(\mu\) of \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_1\) satisfies \(\mu\le a_k\), and every blue parent of \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_2\) satisfies \(\mu\ge b_k\). The same three arguments apply.

*(S3), both cuts.* By Step 2 and the subproblem formulas, the parents of \(\Sigma\cap I_R\), in walk order, are those of \(\Sigma_1\cap I^1_R\cap\alpha_1\), which are weakly increasing in \(<_1\), followed by those of \(\Sigma_2\cap I^2_R\cap\alpha_2\), which are weakly increasing and larger. The parents of \(\Sigma\cap I_B\) are those of \(\Sigma_2\cap I^2_B\cap\alpha_2\), weakly decreasing, followed by those of \(\Sigma_1\cap I^1_B\cap\alpha_1\), weakly decreasing and smaller. The slides of Step 1 do not affect (S3). No block is ever reversed: the marker conventions orient each block. ∎

**Proof of Lemma L2.** A *gap* is the run of positions between two cyclically consecutive selected halfedges. A gap is *internal* when those halfedges are consecutive halfedges of one bank; it is then a single position. Choose the markers as follows.

- *Both colors are selected.* By (H1), exactly two gaps change color. Put \(t_R\) anywhere in the blue-to-red gap and \(t_B\) anywhere in the red-to-blue gap. A single-position gap between halfedges of different colors is not pure, by L2.1. A gap containing a halfedge contains that halfedge's mixed endpoint. So both markers are admissible. Banks are monochromatic, so neither gap is internal.
- *One color is selected.* By (H2), either some boundary halfedge is unselected, and its gap is not internal and contains its mixed endpoint, or two consecutive banks meet at a mixed position, which is a non-internal gap. Put both markers at that mixed position, with the flag that empties the interval of the absent color.
- *Nothing is selected.* Put both markers at any mixed position, with either tie flag.

The color condition holds, so Lemma L2.4 applies.

- *Local feasibility and exposure.* Joins and selected halfedges on one target edge share its color. So (S1) and (S2) give pairwise nonalternating flips and exposure on each target edge.
- *Monotonicity.* No marker lies in an internal gap. Hence each bank is a contiguous piece of the interval of its color. By (S3) its parents are weakly monotone along \(W\). They are distinct, so they are strictly monotone.
- *Separation.* Two banks on one target edge are disjoint pieces of the same interval. By (S3), every parent of the earlier piece is at most (along \(I_R\)) or at least (along \(I_B\)) every parent of the later piece. Their parent sets are disjoint by L2.1, so the banks are separated. ∎

**Remark L2b (necessity of (H2)).** (H2) cannot be omitted, even for outward banks. Let \(Q\) be the \(2m\times2n\) rectangle of cells filling \([0,2m]\times[0,2n]\), with all joins, where \(m,n\ge1\). Select its four whole sides as banks; all selected halfedges are outward. The bottom and top sides lie on the lines \(Y=0,2n\) of equal parity, hence on one target edge, and likewise the left and right sides. The partition merging these two edges makes every selected halfedge one color, so (H1) holds. (H2) fails: every halfedge is selected, and the four positions at which consecutive banks meet are the corners \((0,0),(2m,0),(2m,2n),(0,2n)\), all pure.

No order separates both same-edge pairs. Let \(a,b,c,d\) be the corner cells at the top left, top right, bottom right and bottom left. The reflections of the rectangle preserve the two pairs of opposite sides and permute the corners transitively, so we may assume that \(a\) is least among them. Separating the top from the bottom with \(a<c\) puts the top bank first, so \(b<d\). Separating the left from the right with \(a<b\) puts the left bank first, so \(d<b\), a contradiction. For the \(2\times2\) block, an exhaustive check of all 24 orders finds exactly eight that are locally feasible, expose every selected halfedge and are monotone on all four banks; only separation fails. The same check gives 0 valid orders and again eight satisfying the first three conclusions among the 40,320 orders of each of the \(4\times2\) and \(2\times4\) blocks (script `supplement/l2-bank-orders/check_counterexample.py`). Thus outwardness and consecutive merged colors do not by themselves give separation.

**Corollary L2a (configurations used below).** The hypotheses of Lemma L2 hold, without outwardness, in each of the following cases:

(a) at most one bank on each target edge;

(b1) at most four banks, with at most one target edge repeated, and that edge at most twice;

(b2) any number of banks on one target edge and at most one other bank.

These cover every use in Sections 4.3–4.7. Corollary L3a and the remaining regions of L5–L7 are case (a). The common neighbor \(B\) in L5 is case (b1). The region \(F\) with \(m_F\ge2\) in L6, and every region with a repeated bank color in L7, are case (b2).

**Proof.** Each bank is a monochromatic block of the cyclic sequence of selected halfedges.

*(H1).*

- (a) With at most three banks, any two-coloring of at most three cyclic blocks changes color at most twice. With four banks on distinct edges \(e_1e_2e_3e_4\), in cyclic order, the three pairings of the four edges are \(\{e_1e_2\mid e_3e_4\}\), \(\{e_2e_3\mid e_4e_1\}\) and \(\{e_1e_3\mid e_2e_4\}\). Only the last alternates. The two adjacent-pair partitions are distinct pairings, so one of them satisfies (H1).
- (b1) If no edge is repeated, this is case (a). Otherwise let \(e\) be the repeated edge. With at most one further bank there are at most three blocks. With two further banks, on distinct edges \(e',e''\ne e\), one of them, say \(e'\), is adjacent to \(e\). Merge \(e\) with \(e'\). The other class then contains only the block of \(e''\), and both classes are consecutive.
- (b2) All banks on the main edge share a color, and the other bank is a single block.

*(H2).* Suppose (H2) fails for the chosen partition. Then every boundary halfedge is selected, and all lie on the two edges \(e_V,e_H\) of one class. The north side of a topmost cell and the south side of a bottommost cell are then selected on \(e_H\), and they lie in different banks, because a bank has one port. Likewise the west side of a leftmost cell and the east side of a rightmost cell lie in different banks on \(e_V\). So two target edges each carry at least two banks, which all three cases exclude. ∎
