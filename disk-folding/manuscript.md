# Square-grid disks with a surjective consistent cube map fold validly onto the cube

Todd S. Sherman

Research manuscript, 5 November 2026; revised 7, 9 and 14 November 2026

*This manuscript and its computational supplement were written and checked by AI agents under the author's direction, using Anthropic's Fable 5.2 and Opus 5.5, Google's Carbon, and OpenAI's Astra. No human with sufficient expertise in this field of study has reviewed any proof.*

### Abstract

We consider folding a physical disk onto the unit cube, allowing multiple layers and 90- or 180-degree folds along retained grid joins. A physical disk is obtained from finitely many unit squares of the plane grid by gluing some pairs of side-adjacent squares along their common side, and is homeomorphic to a closed disk. We prove that a consistent complete-frame map covering all six cube faces is sufficient for a valid folding in the piecewise-linear ambient-isotopy model. The source, including its slits and corner identifications, is unchanged; the map and layer orders may change. The proof decomposes a map into monochrome disks separated by whole straight crease arcs, constructs a family of maps indexed by arc toggles, and bounds a minimal six-covering member by twelve arcs. An exhaustive certificate on 87,538 abstract colored trees reduces the bound to seven. A geometric ordering argument then realizes the resulting map, and disk topology gives validity. The theorem has no area, interface-length, or retained-dual-rank bound. The proof, including the certificate, also yields an \(O(N^7)\)-time algorithm deciding whether a physical disk with \(N\) squares folds onto the cube, since the candidate crease arcs depend only on the source and at most seven are needed. Both results concern disk sources; they do not settle the corresponding existence and complexity questions for sources with holes.

## Introduction

A face assignment can satisfy every square-edge incidence and still fail to describe a realizable layered folding. Conversely, an unrealizable prescribed map does not show that its source cannot fold: another map may admit compatible layers. The distinction between a particular map and the existence of some valid map is central here.

**Theorem A.** Let \(P\) be a physical disk, that is, a physical source in the sense of Section 1.1 that is homeomorphic to a closed disk, with its retained unit-edge joins, slits, and corner identifications fixed. Then \(P\) admits a valid folding onto the unit cube in the layered PL model if and only if it admits a consistent complete-frame map covering all six cube faces. The sufficient direction introduces no source cuts, gluings, or additional crease lines.

Here a valid folding is, as in Aichholzer, Lehner, and Lindorfer [ALL25, Definition 3.2], a folded state that is ambient isotopic to a trivial folding, that is, to a realization of a flat blueprint. It does not mean a motion in which square panels remain rigid at every intermediate time. Section 1 makes the model precise.

The necessity is immediate. For sufficiency, a 90-degree interface continues straight through each interior vertex and cannot cross another such interface. Whole interfaces cut the disk conceptually into a tree of monochrome disks. Reflecting one side in a symmetry fixing the target cube edge toggles that interface while preserving every retained join. A minimum six-covering member of this finite family has exclusive leaf colors, at most four leaves, and at most twelve active interfaces. Four leaves reduce analytically to five interfaces; the remaining trees are paths or Y-shaped trees. A complete certificate reduces all their possibilities to at most seven interfaces. Section 4 constructs compatible layer orders for these remaining arrangements. Section 1 turns them into an embedded PL disk and hence a valid folding.

The distinction between an interface and a unit join is essential. One interface may contain arbitrarily many joins, and each monochrome region may have arbitrarily many squares. The construction treats the complete sequences of bank ports, not a replacement by single hinges.

### Relation to previous work

Aichholzer et al. [ABDD18] compare folding models and give algorithms for tree-shaped sources, and Czajkowski et al. [CDD+20] study small polyominoes in models with additional creases, such as the half-grid model. Classifications in such models are not assertions in the fixed-grid model considered here. Table 1 of [CDD+20] summarizes the status of cube folding of polyominoes across folding models as of 2020; for the grid model with orthogonal creases and polyominoes whose retained cell-adjacency graphs may contain cycles, it records the problem as partially characterized and open. Czajkowski et al. also report [CDD+20, Section 3] that Dunham and Whieldon [DW18] found by exhaustive search all solutions of Gardner's puzzle, which asks to cut slits into a \(3\times3\) square so that it folds along grid lines into a unit cube with only one side of the paper showing on the outside, and also all solutions without this last condition.

Aichholzer et al. [AAAC21] study polyominoes with holes, use consistent mappings as a necessary test for foldability, and ask [AAAC21, Section 5] whether this necessary condition is also sufficient. Aichholzer, Lehner, and Lindorfer [ALL25] give the PL validity framework used here and exhibit prescribed-map obstructions. In particular, their Example 4.5 is a surjective consistent mapping of a simply connected polyomino that is realized by no folded state, so the sufficient direction of Theorem A may have to change the map. They ask in Question 4.9 whether some polyomino admits surjective pseudo-foldings but no valid one. Lemma B1 in Section 1.6 identifies the polyominoes of [ALL25] (squares identified only along edges) with physical sources, their simply connected polyominoes with physical disks, and their consistent mappings, read as isometric on each square, with consistent complete-frame maps. With this identification, Theorem A shows that no simply connected polyomino admits surjective pseudo-foldings without admitting a valid one (Corollary A1 in Section 5). It does not settle Question 4.9 for polyominoes with holes. Lehner and Shirley [LS25] study cooperating holes in rectangular sources, outside the disk class.

Open Problem 9.2 of [ALL25] asks for a complete characterization of the simply connected polyominoes that can be folded into a cube; there "into" is to be read as "onto", since in the sense of [ALL25, Definition 3.3] every polyomino folds into the cube trivially, by 180-degree folds onto one face [ALL25, Section 4.1, p. 613]. For these polyominoes Theorem A reduces the problem to the existence of a surjective consistent complete-frame map. It gives no characterization in terms of shape. Conjecture 4.10 of [ALL25] states that CubeFolding, the problem of deciding whether a given polyomino folds onto the cube, can be solved in polynomial time. For tree-shaped polyominoes this follows from the classification of [ALL25, Section 7], as Lehner and Shirley note [LS25, Section 1]. Corollary A2 in Section 5 settles the simply connected case: the candidate crease arcs, maximal straight runs of retained joins through full stars, depend only on the source, and by Lemma R1 and Proposition C3 at most seven of them are needed, so CubeFolding for simply connected polyominoes with \(N\) squares can be decided in \(O(N^7)\) time. Like Theorem A, this relies on the finite claim C2 behind Proposition C3, which is checked by computer (Section 3.2). The conjecture remains open for polyominoes with holes.

Two special cases of Theorem A were known. First, tree-shaped polyominoes, those whose retained cell-adjacency graph is a tree, are physical disks, and Aichholzer, Lehner, and Lindorfer [ALL25, Section 7] classify the tree-shaped polyominoes that fold onto the cube. This extends [ABDD18, Theorem 4], which they describe as a characterization, in the grid model, of the foldable tree-shaped polyominoes whose bounding box has height at most 3; they note that its statement and proof appear to be incomplete and give their own formulation and proof. Their arguments for non-foldability show that the trees concerned have no surjective consistent mapping. For \(3\times3\) and \(3\times4\) bounding boxes they rely on computer-generated lists of minimal foldable trees [ALL25, Figures 25–26]. We checked by computer that, up to symmetry, these lists consist exactly of the trees of those bounding sizes that admit a surjective consistent mapping but contain no proper sub-polyomino admitting one. Hence, granted the correctness of these lists, the tree case of Theorem A follows from [ALL25, Section 7]. Second, [ALL25, Theorem 8.2] shows that a simply connected polyomino folds onto the cube if some boundary point has horizontal and vertical distance at least 3 from one of its valid corners, in the sense defined there. This covers many disks that are not trees. It does not imply Theorem A, since some non-tree disks with a surjective consistent map fail its hypothesis; the smallest of these have eight cells. Theorem A covers all physical disks, whose retained cell-adjacency graphs may contain many cycles.

We know of no individual disk whose foldability needs the reduction of Sections 2–4, and none is presented here as a new example. An exhaustive computer search over the non-tree disks that have a surjective consistent map but fail the hypothesis of [ALL25, Theorem 8.2], with at most 12 cells and all joins retained or with at most 10 cells and slits allowed, finds that each of them also folds by more direct means: all but 49 by simple folds in the sense of Arkin et al. [ABD+04], which reduce the disk to a layered cube net that is then folded onto the cube, and the remaining 49 by wrapping a layered tree-shaped stack around the cube, a step whose validity rests on Theorem M3 and Corollary D1. Our computations in this and the preceding paragraph bear only on the relation to previous work; Theorem A does not depend on them, and their scripts are not part of the supplement. The contribution of Theorem A is the statement for all physical disks, not an individual example.

The bank-order lemma, Lemma L2 of Section 4.2, is proved directly, by induction along straight runs of joins. Its topological inputs are the classification of compact PL surfaces and the arc-splitting statement (Sch) of Section 4.2, which follows from the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem [MT01, Section 2.2] and the \(\theta\)-graph lemma [MT01, Section 2.1]. The proof of Lemma R1 in Section 2 uses the same inputs. All geometric reductions are proved below. Section 1 compares its local order condition and its realization theorem with results on flat folded states [ADK26], on folding motions of polygonal paper [DDMO04], and on lifting maps to embeddings [Mel17]. Section 6 gives a supporting example showing why requiring each face's monochrome square subgraph to be connected is too restrictive.

The computational step checks positive witnesses over a proved exhaustive abstract domain. It is separated from the source reduction and geometric realization, so a reviewer can examine the finite claim without trusting the search procedure that found its certificates.

## 1. Source complexes, ordered maps, and PL realizations

This section fixes the folding model and proves the geometric implication used by the finite certificates: feasible edge-layer orders give an embedded realization, and for a physical disk that realization is valid. The realization lemma is proved here; it is not inferred from agreement between two programs.

### 1.1 Physical sources and complete frames

Let \(S\subset\mathbb Z^2\) be a nonempty finite set of distinct cells. Start with a disjoint copy of the closed unit square for each \(s\in S\). For any selected pair of side-adjacent cells, identify their common sides by the identification induced by their positions in the plane. Call these the **retained joins**. Nonselected adjacencies are slits. Make no further identifications: in particular, coincident drawn corners are identified only when the retained side identifications imply this transitively. Let \(P\) be the resulting quotient and assume its retained cell-adjacency graph is connected. We call \(P\) a **physical source**. Every identification is induced by planar positions, so the map \(\pi:P\to\mathbb R^2\) sending each square onto its cell is well defined; we call it the **planar position map**.

Every physical source is a compact, orientable PL surface with nonempty boundary. Indeed, corners are identified only through retained joins, and a join identifies corners over the same lattice point. Hence the corners forming one abstract vertex make up a **corner fan**: consecutive sectors of the four-sector planar star at a lattice point, joined across the retained sides between them. Its link is an interval, or a circle when the fan has all four sectors and all four intervening joins; we then call the vertex a **full star**. In particular, no corner pinch occurs. The orientations of the squares induced from the plane are compatible across every retained join, and a cell of \(S\) maximizing \(x+y\) has its top and right sides in \(\partial P\). A **physical disk**, or **disk source**, is a physical source homeomorphic to a closed disk. A disk certificate must verify the actual quotient, not the apparent outline of the drawing. Connectivity, orientability, vertex-link conditions, Euler characteristic one, and one boundary component suffice to recognize a disk in this class.

Write \(C=\partial[0,1]^3\). Number the corners of a source square counterclockwise by \(0,1,2,3\), starting at its bottom-left corner, and number side \(p\) from corner \(p\) to corner \(p+1\pmod4\). A **complete frame** assigns to these corners four ordered vertices of a cube face and determines the affine isometry of the whole square. It may be written

\[
\beta_i(x,y)=o_i+x u_i+y v_i,
\qquad 0\leq x,y\leq1,
\]

where \(u_i,v_i\) are orthogonal signed coordinate vectors and the image is one face of \(C\). Its outward face normal is denoted \(n_i\); this normal is determined by the target face, independently of the orientation of \((u_i,v_i)\).

A **consistent map** is a complete frame for every square such that, for every retained join \((i,p,j,q)\), meaning that side \(p\) of square \(i\) is identified with side \(q=p+2\pmod4\) of the square \(j\) across it, the two frames agree at the identified corners: writing \(c_p\) for corner \(p\) of \([0,1]^2\),

\[
\beta_i(c_p)=\beta_j(c_{q+1}),\qquad
\beta_i(c_{p+1})=\beta_j(c_q).
\]

Thus the affine maps descend to a continuous map \(\beta:P\to C\). Matching only face labels would not suffice. The map is **surjective** when all six cube faces occur. At a retained join the two images are either the same face, called a 180-degree join, or the two different faces incident with its target cube edge, called a 90-degree join. No third possibility exists for two cube faces containing that complete edge.

For each target face, prescribe a strict total order of its source squares. Write \(r_i\) for the rank, from \(0\) to \(m_f-1\), of square \(i\) among the \(m_f\) squares assigned to face \(f\), increasing away from the cube. Orders on different target faces are independent.

### 1.2 The local order condition

Fix a cube edge \(E\), incident with faces \(f,g\). Each retained join mapped to \(E\) pairs two square occurrences, each on one of these faces. List the \(f\)-occurrences in increasing rank and the \(g\)-occurrences in decreasing rank around a circle. Require that no two pairs have alternating endpoints in this cyclic order. Occurrences not used by a retained join at \(E\) need not be paired. A square cannot supply two different sides at the same target cube edge, so distinct pairs have distinct occurrences. The condition can equivalently be checked by three kinds of comparisons:

1. Two joins between different target faces preserve relative rank order on the two faces.
2. The endpoint of such a join is not strictly between the endpoints of a same-face join on that face.
3. The rank intervals of two same-face joins on the same target face are disjoint or nested, not alternating.

Same-face joins on opposite incident faces impose no additional pairwise condition. These cases enumerate all ways two pairs can use the two face intervals. We call the total orders **locally feasible** when this condition holds at every cube edge. The following proof establishes the sufficiency we need; it does not assume that a local test determines validity for sources with holes.

Comparisons 1, 2 and 3 are the cube-edge analogues of the tortilla-tortilla, taco-tortilla and taco-taco constraints for flat folded states, respectively. Akitaya, Demaine, and Ku [ADK26, Theorem 1] show that a flat folding of a face-convex crease pattern admits a valid layer order defined by such facewise constraints exactly when it admits a valid pointwise layer order. Their setting is flat and has no cube edges, so it does not imply Lemma M1 or Theorem M3.

### 1.3 An explicit transverse construction

**Lemma M1 (disjoint transverse connectors).** For locally feasible ranks on a source with \(N\) squares, all joins at one cube edge can be realized simultaneously by pairwise disjoint polygonal arcs in its outward transverse quadrant. Their endpoints are at explicitly chosen strictly increasing heights on the two outward-normal rays. The arcs meet these rays only at their endpoints and can be scaled arbitrarily small.

**Proof.** Choose \(A>N^2\) and \(t>0\). Place the occurrence of square \(i\) at height

\[
h_i=tA(r_i+1).
\]

A join between different faces is the straight segment between its two ray endpoints. A same-face join with ranks \(a,b\) uses a rectangular U: starting on its normal ray, move a distance \(t\left|a-b\right|\) in the direction of the other face's outward normal (parallel to the other ray), move parallel to the first ray to the other height, and return to that ray. All non-endpoint points have positive coordinates in the outward quadrant. Each path lies within transverse distance \(\sqrt2\,tAN\) of the cube edge.

There are four pair types. Two straight connectors are disjoint because their intercepts on both positive axes have the same strict order. Two U-connectors on the same ray are disjoint if their rank intervals are disjoint; if one interval properly contains the other, its excursion \(t\left|a-b\right|\) is strictly larger, so its two legs and outside segment avoid the inner U. U-connectors on opposite rays are disjoint because their axial heights are at least \(tA\), whereas their excursions are less than \(tN<tA\).

Finally consider a straight connector and a U on the first ray. The straight connector's intercept cannot lie inside the U's rank interval. If it lies below that interval, their first-coordinate ranges are disjoint. If it lies above the interval, write its intercept as \(tAk\), its other intercept as \(tA\ell\), and let \(x\) lie in the U's axial interval. Distinct integral ranks imply \(x\leq tA(k-1)\), so the straight segment has second coordinate

\[
tA\ell\left(1-\frac{x}{tAk}\right)
\geq\frac{tA\ell}{k}
\geq\frac{tA}{N}
>tN.
\]

The entire U has second coordinate less than \(tN\). The other-ray case is identical. This exhausts all pairs, and all separations are strict. Scaling \(t\) proves the last assertion. \(\square\)

### 1.4 Filling vertex neighborhoods

**Lemma M2 (simultaneous relative caps).** Let \(B\) be a PL 3-ball. A finite pairwise disjoint collection of simple PL circles and simple PL arcs on \(\partial B\) bounds pairwise disjoint PL disks in \(B\) as follows: a circle is the entire boundary of its disk; an arc is one boundary arc of its disk, with the complementary boundary arc free in the interior of \(B\). Each disk meets \(\partial B\) exactly in its prescribed circle or arc.

**Proof.** Use a thin inward PL collar \(\partial B\times[0,\delta]\); for the cubical balls below, it can be obtained by triangulating the boundary and the prisms between two nested cubes. Choose a point of \(\partial B\) outside the prescribed curves and identify the punctured sphere with the plane. Each circle bounds the closed Jordan disk avoiding that point. These finitely many disks are laminar: any two are disjoint or one is contained in the interior of the other.

After a common subdivision, refined (for example barycentrically) so that each prescribed curve is a full subcomplex, that is, no simplex outside the curve has all its vertices on it, represent each circle cap as the graph in the collar of a PL function on its Jordan disk, zero on the boundary and strictly positive in the interior; for instance, take the function that is linear on simplices, zero at the vertices of the circle, and positive at all other vertices. Choose functions from outer disks to inner disks. The function already chosen for a containing disk has a positive minimum on each compact child disk. Choose the child's positive height smaller than this minimum. Thus, at every point where two graph domains overlap, the containing cap lies strictly deeper. The resulting caps are pairwise disjoint and meet the sphere only in their prescribed circles.

Choose disjoint small one-sided rectangular neighborhoods of the arc components on the sphere, avoiding every circle. Each arc is one boundary side of its neighborhood. Since a connected such neighborhood avoids all circles, it lies either wholly inside or wholly outside each circle's Jordan disk. Realize its half-disk as the graph of a PL function that is zero precisely on the prescribed arc and positive elsewhere, including on the complementary boundary arc except its endpoints. For each containing circle cap, its depth has a positive minimum on the chosen small neighborhood; choose the arc cap shallower than all these minima. The neighborhoods of distinct arcs are disjoint. This gives the required simultaneous caps. \(\square\)

The lemma requires disjoint *simple* sphere links. In the following application this is proved before capping, from the embedding outside the vertex balls and the actual interval/circle links of the source. It would not follow merely from an Euler characteristic calculation. Coning every sphere link to a common center would also not prove the lemma.

### 1.5 Realization with a pointwise error bound

**Theorem M3 (ordered-map realization).** Let \(P\) be a physical source (Section 1.1) with \(N\) squares. Every consistent complete-frame map with locally feasible face orders has, for every positive \(\varepsilon\), a PL embedding \(F:P\to\mathbb R^3\) with

\[
\lVert F(x)-\beta(x)\rVert<\varepsilon\qquad(x\in P),
\]

whose face patches occur in the prescribed layer order.

**Proof.** We first construct the image pieces and then give their source parametrization. Choose

\[
0<\rho<\min(1/10,\varepsilon/20),\qquad
0<\eta<\rho/10,
\]

and choose \(A>N^2\) and \(t>0\) so small that \(D=tAN<\rho/10\). Around each cube vertex \(v\), remove the interior of the axis-aligned cubical ball

\[
B_v=v+[-\rho,\rho]^3.
\]

These balls are pairwise disjoint. Translate the image of source square \(i\) by \(h_i n_i\), retaining the part outside the open vertex balls. Its tangential coordinates still run over its target unit face. Different patches on one face are in different planes. Patches on different faces are disjoint: each stays in its own face's tangential square and is strictly outside in its normal coordinate.

At each retained join, insert the product of its Lemma M1 transverse arc with the longitudinal interval \([\rho,1-\rho]\) of its target cube edge. Bind its endpoints to the two face patches using the complete edge frames. This makes a polygonal ribbon with the prescribed, untwisted source identification. The ribbon's interior is strictly in the outward transverse quadrant, whereas incident face-patch interiors are on the inward side of an axis; hence it meets the patches on the two incident faces only in its intended attaching intervals. It misses the patches on the other four faces. Such a patch has normal coordinate at least \(1+tA\) or at most \(-tA\). On the ribbon, this coordinate is longitudinal, in \([\rho,1-\rho]\), for the two faces perpendicular to the edge; for the two faces opposite the incident ones it is transverse, in \([1,1+D]\) or \([-D,0]\). Lemma M1 makes ribbons at the same cube edge disjoint. Ribbons at different edges are disjoint as well: their transverse coordinates are at most \(D<\rho/10\), while their longitudinal coordinates remain at least \(\rho\) from the endpoints. This separates even edge neighborhoods incident with the same cube vertex. Edges not sharing a vertex have a still larger separation. We have obtained an embedded PL surface outside the open balls.

On each \(\partial B_v\), the cut face patches and ribbons form a disjoint collection of simple polygonal curves and arcs. More explicitly, a translated square corner supplies its cut corner arc, and each incident retained join supplies the connector between the appropriate arc endpoints. These connect in exactly the actual source corner fan order. No corner is identified just because its drawing or target vertex coincides with another. A closed four-sector fan gives a circle; a boundary fan gives an arc. Distinct fans have disjoint links because all the face and ribbon pieces are already embedded. Apply Lemma M2 inside each ball. Its interiors are disjoint from the previously constructed exterior, and its prescribed boundaries attach to the correct fans. The union is an embedded surface with exactly the source incidence.

It remains to ensure that this is a realization of the **whole source with pointwise control**, rather than merely an unparametrized nearby surface. Along each retained source edge take a collar of width \(\eta\) on each side, over the longitudinal range \([\rho,1-\rho]\), so that it stops at the source corner neighborhoods. Since \(\eta<\rho\), these collars are pairwise disjoint. Keep the longitudinal parameter fixed. In the transverse collar parameter, map the source interval \([-\eta,\eta]\) by a monotone piecewise-linear map onto the concatenation of the first face strip of width \(\eta\), the complete connector, and the second face strip of width \(\eta\), agreeing with the translated affine face maps at \(\pm\eta\). The image consists of the full face strips of width \(\eta\) and the ribbon; only the source parameter interval of each face collar is compressed, to make room for the connector. This gives a homeomorphism of the original collar onto the patch-and-ribbon neighborhood. Thus inserting a ribbon has not introduced extra source material or changed its gluing. Outside these collars use the translated affine face map.

Choose the remaining source corner neighborhoods with frontier assembled from the preimages of the face-patch truncation arcs under the translated affine face maps and the source edge-collar intervals at their longitudinal endpoints. The target truncation occurs at longitudinal coordinate \(\rho\), and the preceding reparametrizations change only the transverse source coordinate within width \(\eta\). Its source frontier therefore lies within tangential distance \(\rho+\eta<2\rho\) of the relevant corner in every incident square; fill in the corresponding actual corner fan on that side. Each resulting source corner neighborhood is contained in the corner square of side \(2\rho\). Its frontier is the fan interval or circle already parametrized on \(\partial B_v\). For a closed fan, extend the PL homeomorphism of this frontier onto the prescribed circle to a PL homeomorphism of the corner neighborhood onto its Lemma M2 cap by coning (the PL Alexander trick). For a boundary fan, first extend the homeomorphism of the frontier arc onto the prescribed arc by any PL homeomorphism between the complementary boundary arcs, the one in \(\partial P\) and the free arc of the cap, and then cone. These extensions agree with the adjacent collar maps on every prescribed attaching interval. They define a PL homeomorphism from \(P\) onto the constructed embedded surface.

For completeness, the error estimate separates three regions. Off source edge collars and corner neighborhoods, the displacement is \(h_i\leq D\). In an edge collar, the source and image retain the same longitudinal coordinate; the source blueprint is within \(\eta\) of the target edge, and the corresponding image is within \(\eta+\sqrt2D\). Hence their distance is at most \(2\eta+\sqrt2D\). In a source corner neighborhood, \(\beta(x)\) is within \(2\sqrt2\rho\) of its target cube vertex, and its cap image is within \(\sqrt3\rho\). Their distance is therefore less than \(5\rho<\varepsilon\). The other two bounds are smaller. This proves the required pointwise inequality and preserves the prescribed strict order on the unmodified face interiors. \(\square\)

The construction allows caps inside the cube. An outside-cube constraint is neither assumed nor supplied. The face orders are witnessed on the substantial parallel face patches; the theorem makes no uniqueness claim about all embeddings near the same blueprint. No choice of pairwise linking numbers or of a link-simplification heuristic enters the proof.

### 1.6 Relation to the published folding model and disk validity

Aichholzer, Lehner, and Lindorfer [ALL25, Section 3, pp. 604–605, and Definitions 3.1–3.3] define a folding blueprint of an abstract polyomino as a map to \(\mathbb R^3\) that is an isometry on each square, a folded state as a PL embedding, and \(\varepsilon\)-correspondence by the pointwise inequality used in Theorem M3. A blueprint is flat when its image lies in a plane and the images of distinct squares overlap only in edges. A polyomino is an abstract polyomino admitting a flat blueprint, and a trivial folding is a realization of a flat blueprint (Definition 3.1). Validity means ambient isotopy to a trivial folding (Definition 3.2). A folding is **onto** the cube when its blueprint is surjective (Definition 3.3). In particular, the realization itself need not lie exactly on the cube surface, and the ambient isotopy is not a rigid-hinge motion constrained to preserve square shapes at intermediate times.

Their Theorem 3.4 states, for a folded state of a polyomino, the equivalence of validity, containment of the embedded surface in a polyhedral sphere, and an unlink boundary. We use only its disk case.

To apply these results we fix how the objects of [ALL25] correspond to those of Section 1.1. In [ALL25, p. 604], an abstract polyomino is obtained from disjoint unit squares by identifying some of their edges in pairs. We read this, as described there, with vertices identified only through these edge identifications; this is also the reading under which the boundary of a folded state is a disjoint union of circles, as noted in [ALL25, p. 605]. If corners could also be identified without an edge identification, as the square-complex formulation in [ALL25, p. 604] would permit, the vertex so formed would have a disconnected link, and the complex would be neither a surface nor a physical source; Lemma B1 and its consequences use the edge-only reading. A polyomino of [ALL25] consists of finitely many squares, at least one, joined edge to edge into a connected whole [ALL25, Section 2]; we read Definition 3.1, whose wording does not repeat these conditions, with them, so that the complex \(P\) is finite, nonempty and connected. In the plane figure of a polyomino, a **hole** is a maximal connected set of missing squares and slits contained in the interior of a cycle of the dual graph \(D(P)\) [ALL25, p. 604], and a polyomino is **simply connected** when it has no hole [ALL25, p. 603]. A **consistent mapping** [ALL25, p. 607] is a homomorphism of polygonal complexes \(P\to C\): it maps faces to faces, edges to edges, and vertices to vertices, preserving incidence. Aichholzer, Lehner, and Lindorfer present consistent mappings as a combinatorial version of folding blueprints that carries the same information, and we read them in this sense, as isometric on each square: the mapping carries the corners and sides of each square bijectively, preserving incidence, onto the corners and sides of one face of \(C\). A **pseudo-folding** adds a layer map to a consistent mapping and is surjective when the mapping is [ALL25, p. 608].

**Lemma B1 (agreement with [ALL25]).** Let \(P\) be a physical source with planar position map \(\pi\).

(a\) The map \(\pi\) is a flat blueprint, so \(P\) is a polyomino in the sense of [ALL25, Definition 3.1]. Conversely, every polyomino in this sense, read as above, is isomorphic to a physical source by a map that is an isometry on each square.

(b\) \(P\) is simply connected in the sense of [ALL25] if and only if it is a physical disk.

(c\) A map \(\beta:P\to C\) is a folding blueprint into \(C\) if and only if it is a consistent complete-frame map, that is, an affine isometry of each square onto one face of \(C\) satisfying the join equations; it is surjective exactly when all six faces occur. Correspondingly, the consistent mappings of [ALL25], read as above, are exactly the maps of faces, edges, and vertices induced by consistent complete-frame maps, and such a mapping, or a pseudo-folding containing it, is surjective exactly when all six faces occur.

**Proof.** *Part (a).* The source \(P\) is obtained from disjoint unit squares by identifying sides in pairs, and its vertices are identified only through these side identifications (Section 1.1). Regarded as a map into a plane of \(\mathbb R^3\), the map \(\pi\) is an isometry on each square, and the images of distinct squares are distinct cells, which meet at most in a side or a corner. Thus \(\pi\) is a flat blueprint. Conversely, let \(\pi\) be a flat blueprint of a polyomino \(P\) of [ALL25], regarded as a map to the plane containing its image. Since \(\pi\) is injective on each square, no two distinct points of one square are identified in \(P\). Hence, if an edge \(e\) of a square \(A\) is identified with an edge of a square \(B\), then \(B\neq A\), and \(\pi(A)\) and \(\pi(B)\) are unit squares with the common side \(\pi(e)\). They are not equal, since images of distinct squares overlap only in edges, so \(\pi(B)\) is the reflection of \(\pi(A)\) in \(\pi(e)\). Identified points have equal images, so the two copies of \(e\) are identified as these positions induce. As \(P\) is connected and its vertices are identified only through edges, any two squares are linked by a chain of edge identifications. In plane coordinates in which one square maps onto \([0,1]^2\), every square therefore maps onto a cell of \(\mathbb Z^2\), and distinct squares map onto distinct cells. Let \(S\) be the set of these cells, which is finite and nonempty, and retain exactly the joins between side-adjacent cells of \(S\) along which \(P\) identifies edges. Two cells share at most one side, and each edge is identified with at most one other edge, so this is a selection of joins as in Section 1.1, and its retained cell-adjacency graph is connected. In both complexes, vertices are identified only through the edge identifications. Hence \(\pi\) induces an isomorphism of \(P\) onto the resulting physical source that is an isometry on each square.

*Part (b).* By (a), the plane figure of [ALL25, Section 2] is \(\pi(P)\); the flat blueprint is unique up to isometry [ALL25, p. 605]. Its squares are the cells of \(S\), its missing squares are the cells of \(\mathbb Z^2\) not in \(S\), and its slits are the common sides of side-adjacent cells of \(S\) without a retained join. The dual graph \(D=D(P)\) is drawn with a vertex at the center of each cell of \(S\) and, for each retained join, the unit segment between the two centers; it is a connected plane graph contained in the lines through cell centers. Each edge of \(D\) lies in the union of its two cells and meets their boundaries only at the midpoint of their common, retained side. A closed missing square or slit meets the cells of \(S\) only in their boundaries and contains no such midpoint, so it misses \(D\) and lies in one face of \(D\). For a lattice point \(v\), the open square \(Q_v=v+(-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)^2\) misses \(D\). It is a face of \(D\) exactly when its four sides are edges of \(D\), that is, when the four cells at \(v\) lie in \(S\) and the four sides at \(v\) are retained, so that \(v\) carries a full star; if a side of \(Q_v\) is not an edge of \(D\), its interior misses \(D\) and connects \(Q_v\) to a neighboring square. Such a face \(Q_v\) contains no point of a missing square or slit, since it meets only the four cells and the four sides at \(v\). Every other bounded face \(\Phi\) contains such a point. Indeed, \(\Phi\) is open and the lines through cell centers have empty interior, so \(\Phi\) meets some \(Q_v\), and contains it, as \(Q_v\) is connected and misses \(D\). Then \(v\) does not carry a full star, since otherwise \(\Phi=Q_v\). So a cell at \(v\) is missing or a side at \(v\) is a slit; it meets \(Q_v\subset\Phi\) and therefore lies in \(\Phi\).

Let \(N\), \(R\), and \(n\) be the numbers of squares, retained joins, and full stars of \(P\). The vertices of \(P\) are its corner fans. Over one lattice point they are the components of the graph whose vertices are the cells of \(S\) at that point and whose edges are the retained sides at it. This graph is a subgraph of a 4-cycle, so it has as many components as it has cells minus retained sides, plus one when it is the whole 4-cycle. Summing over lattice points, \(P\) has \(4N-2R+n\) vertices; it has \(N\) faces and \(4N-R\) edges, so \(\chi(P)=N-R+n\). By Euler's formula, the connected plane graph \(D\), with \(N\) vertices and \(R\) edges, has \(1-N+R\) bounded faces, and \(n\) of them are the squares \(Q_v\) of full stars. Hence \(\chi(P)=1-m\), where \(m\) is the number of the other bounded faces. As \(P\) is a compact connected orientable surface with nonempty boundary (Section 1.1), \(\chi(P)\leq1\), with equality exactly when \(P\) is a disk. Thus \(P\) is a physical disk if and only if \(m=0\).

If \(P\) has a hole, then a missing square or slit lies inside a cycle of \(D\). Its face lies inside that cycle, so it is bounded and is not the square of a full star, and \(m>0\). Conversely, let \(m>0\). Choose a bounded face \(\Phi\) other than the squares of full stars, a missing square or slit \(x\) in \(\Phi\), and a point \(y\) of \(x\). Let \(Z\) be the set of edges of \(D\) with \(\Phi\) on exactly one side. Around each vertex of \(D\), the sectors between consecutive edges enter and leave \(\Phi\) an even number of times, so every vertex has even degree in \(Z\), and \(Z\) is an edge-disjoint union of cycles. Take a ray from \(y\) that avoids the vertices of \(D\) and is parallel to none of its edges. It eventually lies in the unbounded face, and it passes between \(\Phi\) and other faces exactly when it crosses an edge of \(Z\). So it crosses \(Z\), and hence one of these cycles, an odd number of times. By the parity criterion for polygons (see the polygonal Jordan curve theorem in [MT01, Section 2.1]), \(y\) lies inside that cycle. The maximal connected union of missing squares and slits containing \(x\) misses \(D\), so it lies inside the same cycle, and \(P\) has a hole.

*Part (c).* Let \(\beta\) be a folding blueprint into \(C\). An isometry of a square into \(\mathbb R^3\) is the restriction of an affine map, so for each square \(s\) the image \(\beta(s)\) is a planar unit square in \(C\). The six closed faces of \(C\) cover it, so one of them meets it in a set with nonempty interior relative to its plane. That plane is then the plane of this face, whose intersection with \(C\) is the face itself. Hence \(\beta(s)\) is this face, and \(\beta\) restricted to \(s\) is an affine isometry onto it carrying corners to corners, that is, a complete frame. The identified points of a retained join have equal images; at its two identified corners, this is the pair of join equations of Section 1.1. Conversely, a consistent complete-frame map descends to a continuous map on \(P\) (Section 1.1) that is an isometry on each square, so it is a folding blueprint into \(C\). Its image is the union of the faces that occur. For a consistent mapping read as above, the incidence-preserving bijection on a square determines the corner images of exactly one complete frame, and every complete frame arises in this way. At a retained join, the identified side and its two identified corners have one image each, which is again the pair of join equations; conversely, the join equations make the induced maps of faces, edges, and vertices well defined on \(P\). Since every edge and vertex of \(C\) lies in a face, such a mapping, and a pseudo-folding containing it, is surjective exactly when all six faces occur. \(\square\)

The nondegenerate reading in (c) is needed. A homomorphism of polygonal complexes in the literal sense may fold the boundary of a square onto a path of cube edges. With such degenerate squares, the \(1\times6\) strip, a physical disk, has a homomorphism onto all six faces, twelve edges, and eight vertices of \(C\). It has no surjective consistent complete-frame map: adjacent squares of a strip share a side, so all its frames send the direction of the shared sides to one cube axis, and only four faces are parallel to that axis. Aichholzer, Lehner, and Lindorfer likewise note that a folding of a \(1\times n\) strip covers at most four faces [ALL25, p. 621]. Under the literal reading, the strip would thus admit a surjective pseudo-folding but no folding onto \(C\), and Question 4.9 of [ALL25] would have a trivial positive answer.

**Corollary D1 (disk certificate).** If \(P\) is a physical disk, a surjective consistent map and locally feasible face orders certify a valid folding of \(P\) onto the cube in the [ALL25] model.

**Proof.** By Lemma B1(a), \(P\) is a polyomino in the sense of [ALL25, Definition 3.1], and by Lemma B1(c), \(\beta\) is a surjective folding blueprint into \(C\). Theorem M3 produces the required folded state, an embedded PL disk, for every sufficiently small prescribed \(\varepsilon\). The boundary of an embedded PL disk is an unknot, which is condition 3 of Theorem 3.4. Alternatively, a sufficiently small one-sided regular neighborhood of the disk is a PL 3-ball whose boundary sphere contains the original disk as one boundary face, which is condition 2. Theorem 3.4 then gives validity, and surjectivity of \(\beta\) gives a folding onto \(C\). \(\square\)

The same argument shows that every folded state of a physical disk is valid; the substance of Corollary D1 is the existence of the realization supplied by Theorem M3. By Lemma B1(b), this validity statement is the disk case of the remark in [ALL25, Section 4.1, p. 611] that a realizable pseudo-folding can fail to be valid only if the polyomino has a hole.

This is a sufficient certificate for an individual map and order. Failure to find orders in a bounded search is not a negative folding result. For sources with holes Theorem M3 still gives an embedded realization, but Corollary D1 no longer applies: its entire boundary link must be addressed separately.

Two related results do not imply Theorem M3 or Corollary D1. In the model of Demaine, Devadoss, Mitchell, and O'Rourke, a folded state of a simple polygon is an isometric map of the paper together with a noncrossing stacking order, and they show that every such state that is well behaved in their sense is reached by a continuous folding motion [DDMO04, Theorem 2]. This is the closest classical analogue of Theorem M3 with Corollary D1, but its conclusion is a motion rather than an \(\varepsilon\)-close PL embedding, a source with slits is not a simple polygon, and its noncrossing conditions at cube edges and vertices would still have to be derived from the local order condition, which is what Lemmas M1 and M2 do. Melikhov's criterion [Mel17, Theorem 5] characterizes the nondegenerate PL maps \(f:X\to Y\) of compact polyhedra that lift to PL embeddings \(X\to Y\times\mathbb R\). Because a lift of \(\beta\) to \(C\times\mathbb R\), placed in a thin bicollar of \(C\) in \(\mathbb R^3\), is an \(\varepsilon\)-realization, the criterion yields Theorem M3 for layer orders that extend continuously over the whole double-point set of \(\beta\), crease points included. It does not yield Theorem M3 in general: the local order condition allows a 180-degree join to wrap around a free side of a square ranked between its two layers, and such an ordered map has no lift to \(C\times\mathbb R\).

Page, section, figure, and item numbers cited from [ALL25] refer to its published version; the arXiv version numbers some of them differently, for example Open Problem 9.2 as Open Problem 8.2.

## 2. A universal reduction to finitely many colored trees

This section concerns complete-frame maps, without prescribing layer orders. All references to cutting are decompositions for the proof: the physical source retains every one of its original joins. A face color is one of the six cube faces; two colors are adjacent when their faces share a cube edge. Write \(F^*\) for the color opposite \(F\). Throughout, \(P\) is the physical disk under consideration. A *vertex* of a physical source is an abstract vertex together with its corner fan (Section 1.1). It is a *full star* when the fan consists of four squares and all four retained joins between them (the circular case of Section 1.1), and a *boundary vertex* otherwise; full stars are interior points of the source, and boundary vertices lie on its boundary.

**Lemma R1 (whole crease arcs).** In a physical disk with a consistent complete-frame map, the retained joins between distinct target faces decompose into straight proper arcs with disjoint interiors. Every unit join on one arc maps to the same complete cube edge. Every interior vertex of an arc is a full star; in particular, consecutive squares on either side of an arc are joined by a retained 180-degree join. Removing these joins from the retained cell-adjacency graph gives monochrome physical disk regions, and their adjacency graph has one edge per arc and is a tree.

**Proof.** At a full star the four square corners have a common image, which is a cube vertex. The four source edge rays therefore map to the three cube edge directions at that vertex, and consecutive rays have different labels because each intervening square maps bijectively onto a face. Up to rotation and relabeling, a proper three-coloring of a four-cycle is either \(A,B,A,B\) or \(A,B,A,C\). The join at a ray is a 180-degree join precisely when the two neighboring ray labels agree. The first pattern has no 90-degree joins; the second has exactly two, on opposite rays labeled \(A\), and 180-degree joins on the rays labeled \(B\) and \(C\). The two rays labeled \(A\) map to the same complete cube edge.

Continue a 90-degree join straight at each full star and stop at every boundary vertex; by the pattern \(A,B,A,C\), the continuation is again a 90-degree join and maps to the same complete cube edge. This convention stops at a boundary fan even if another ray at the same lattice coordinate is collinear. Distinct source vertices are not identified merely because their coordinates agree. Along a run the coordinates advance in one direction on one lattice line. Hence the coordinate map is injective on a run, so a run is finite and cannot close; it ends at two distinct boundary vertices. The open sides of its joins and its interior vertices are interior points of \(P\), and its endpoints lie on \(\partial P\), so each run is a straight proper arc, and all its unit joins map to one complete cube edge. Every 90-degree join lies on exactly one run, because the continuation is determined in both directions. At an interior vertex of a run, the run occupies the two rays labeled \(A\), and the 180-degree joins on the rays labeled \(B\) and \(C\) join the two consecutive squares on either side. That full star carries no other 90-degree join, and an endpoint of a run is not a full star, so distinct runs meet at most in common endpoints. This proves the first three assertions. Let \(\Gamma_1,\ldots,\Gamma_k\) be the runs; they are the arcs.

The source \(P\) is a compact PL surface (Section 1.1) homeomorphic to a closed disk, hence a PL disk by the classification of compact PL surfaces; the same holds for every physical disk. We use the following cutting step. Let \(X\) be a physical disk and \(\Gamma\) a straight run of retained joins of \(X\) whose interior vertices are full stars of \(X\) and whose endpoints are distinct boundary vertices of \(X\). Since its endpoints are boundary vertices, \(\Gamma\) is a maximal such run, that is, a cut of \(X\) in the sense of Section 4.2, and, as for the runs of \(P\) above, it is a proper PL arc of \(X\). By (Sch) of Section 4.2, which is pure topology, derived there from the planar PL Jordan–Schoenflies theorem [MT01, Section 2.2] and the \(\theta\)-graph lemma [MT01, Section 2.1], \(\Gamma\) divides \(X\) into two closed disks meeting exactly in \(\Gamma\). The proof of Part 2 of Lemma L2.2 uses only (Sch), Section 1.1, and the facts that \(X\) is a physical disk and that \(\Gamma\) is a cut of \(X\) forming a proper PL arc with distinct endpoints; the standing hypothesis of Section 4.2 that all squares lie on one face is used there only to call the halves all-flip, and that proof uses nothing from the present section. It shows that the two disks are physical disks \(X_1,X_2\), the *halves*, each formed by the squares it contains and the joins of \(X\) among them, with \(X_1\cup X_2=X\) and \(X_1\cap X_2=\Gamma\). The two squares of each join of \(\Gamma\) lie in different halves, every other join of \(X\) has both squares in one half, and each half is join-connected, that is, connected in its retained cell-adjacency graph. So the halves are the two components of the retained cell-adjacency graph of \(X\) after the joins of \(\Gamma\) are removed. A fan of \(X\) at a vertex off \(\Gamma\) lies unchanged in one half. At a vertex of \(\Gamma\), removing its one join of \(\Gamma\) (at an endpoint, where the fan is a path of squares) or its two joins of \(\Gamma\) (at an interior vertex) splits the fan into two consecutive subfans, one in each half.

Cut \(\Gamma_1,\ldots,\Gamma_k\) in turn. Suppose that \(\Gamma_1,\ldots,\Gamma_j\) have been cut, giving \(j+1\) physical disks, the *pieces*, which are the components of the retained cell-adjacency graph of \(P\) after the joins of these runs are removed. Suppose also that every fan of a piece is a consecutive subfan of a fan of \(P\), and that the fan of \(P\) at a vertex lying on none of these runs is, unchanged, a fan of one piece. All this holds for \(j=0\). Since distinct runs meet at most in common endpoints, which are boundary vertices, the run \(\Gamma=\Gamma_{j+1}\) shares no join with the earlier runs, and its interior vertices are vertices of none of them. So each join of \(\Gamma\) has both squares in one piece, and each interior vertex of \(\Gamma\) is, unchanged, a full star of one piece. Consecutive joins of \(\Gamma\) share such a star, so \(\Gamma\) consists of joins of one piece \(X\), and its interior vertices are full stars of \(X\). At either endpoint of \(\Gamma\), the vertex of \(X\) at which \(\Gamma\) ends has as fan a consecutive subfan of the fan of \(P\) there, which is a path; so it is a boundary vertex of \(X\). The two endpoints have distinct coordinates. The cutting step therefore applies and replaces \(X\) by its two halves, and the inductive hypotheses hold for \(j+1\). In particular, a boundary fan carrying several run endpoints needs no separate treatment: each run splits the current subfan at its own join, and every later run ends at the subfan containing its own last join. After \(k\) cuts there are \(k+1\) pieces; they are exactly the regions of the statement, and each is a physical disk. At a boundary vertex of \(P\), the corresponding vertices of the regions have as fans the maximal intervals of its fan that contain no arc join.

Fix an arc. By the full-star assertion, its squares on one side are consecutive along it and joined by 180-degree joins, which lie on no arc, so they lie in one region. The two sides lay in different halves when the arc was cut, and pieces are only subdivided afterwards, so they lie in different regions. Thus all joins of one arc join the same two regions, and every join between different regions lies on an arc. The adjacency graph, with one edge for each arc, is connected because \(P\) is join-connected, and it has \(k+1\) vertices and \(k\) edges; hence it is a tree, and distinct arcs join distinct pairs of regions. At a boundary vertex of \(P\), consecutive maximal intervals of its fan that contain no arc join are separated by the last join of an arc ending there, and so lie in the two regions that this arc joins. No arc has two joins at that vertex, since the vertex is not an interior vertex of an arc and the endpoints of an arc have distinct coordinates. A tree has no closed walk with pairwise distinct edges, so these intervals lie in pairwise distinct regions. Within a region every retained join is 180 degrees, so all squares map to one face. \(\square\)

For a target edge \(e\), let \(\sigma_e\) be the nonidentity cube symmetry that fixes \(e\) pointwise and exchanges its two incident faces. If those colors are \(A,B\), its face action is \((A\ B)(A^*\ B^*)\); the remaining two colors are fixed. In coordinates based at an endpoint of \(e\), it fixes the edge-axis coordinate and interchanges the other two coordinate distances. This also specifies its action on all cube vertices.

**Lemma R2 (fixed toggle family).** Suppose the initial arc decomposition has \(k\) arcs, and fix the complete frame that the given map assigns to one source square, the root. Call a join originally internal when it lies on no original arc. The consistent maps with this root frame in which every originally internal join is a 180-degree join are exactly \(2^k\) maps, one for each assignment of 90/180 statuses to the original arcs; in each of them every original arc has one status along its whole length. These maps form the fixed toggle family of the given map, and the given map is its member with every original arc at 90 degrees.

**Proof.** Removing one arc separates the region tree and the physical disk into two sides. Apply \(\sigma_e\) to every complete frame on the side not containing the root square. Internal joins on that side remain consistent because both incident frames undergo the same isometry. Every join along the chosen interface retains its endpoint images because \(\sigma_e\) fixes \(e\) pointwise. The interface changes between 90 and 180 degrees; no other retained join changes status.

Every other original arc lies entirely on one side, apart from possible shared boundary endpoints. Its whole target edge therefore remains well-defined after this operation. Root the original tree and process its edges away from the root, toggling the current target edge when needed. This realizes every desired bit vector without changing previously selected bits or the root frame. Different vectors give different maps. Finally, on any connected source, a root complete frame and all join statuses determine at most one map: at a join the parent frame, the two matched endpoints, and the same-face/other-incident-face choice uniquely determine the child's frame. Propagation along a spanning tree proves uniqueness. Conversely, consider any consistent map with the root frame in which every originally internal join is a 180-degree join. By the first paragraph of the proof of Lemma R1, a join on a ray of a full star is a 180-degree join exactly when the labels of its two neighboring rays agree. Opposite rays have the same two neighbors, so at each interior vertex of an original arc the two joins of the arc have equal status, and the status is constant along the arc. The map therefore has the root frame and all join statuses of one constructed member, and it equals that member by uniqueness. \(\square\)

This family is not asserted to contain every consistent map of the source. It is enough that it contains the given surjective map. Choose a surjective member \(M\) minimizing the number \(a\) of active original arcs and contract the inactive edges in the original region tree. The resulting tree \(T\) has \(a+1\) monochrome disk regions and distinct adjacent face colors. Indeed, for every member of the family, the maximal 90-degree runs that Lemma R1 gives for that member are exactly its active original arcs: by Lemma R2 its 90-degree joins are exactly the joins of these arcs, and each original arc is a straight run through full stars whose endpoints are boundary vertices. Contracting the inactive edges of the original region tree therefore gives the region tree of that member itself, since the joins off its active arcs are exactly the joins inside original regions and the joins of its inactive original arcs; the edges of this tree are the whole bend arcs of that member. Hence \(T\) is the region tree of \(M\), and Lemma R1 applied to \(M\) shows that its regions are monochrome physical disks.

**Lemma R3 (exclusive leaves).** Every leaf of \(T\) has a color occurring nowhere else. There are \(\ell\in\{2,3,4\}\) leaves, and precisely \(6-\ell\) colors occur at nonleaf vertices.

**Proof.** Reflect a leaf region by the symmetry fixing its sole attachment edge. Its color becomes its parent's color and the attachment becomes inactive. If the old leaf color occurred elsewhere, coverage would be preserved, contrary to minimality. If the reflected region contains the fixed root, apply the same symmetry globally afterwards. This restores the root frame and does not affect coverage or join statuses. By Lemma R2 the normalized map remains in the original family.

Thus leaf colors are distinct and globally exclusive. Since six colors occur, \(T\) has at least six vertices and has a nonleaf vertex. If there were five leaves, all nonleaves would have the sole remaining color. Nonleaves induce a connected subtree; adjacent vertices have distinct colors, so that subtree would consist of one vertex. This would make a star whose center face is adjacent to five different faces, impossible because a cube face has four neighbors. Six or more leaves leave no available color for a nonleaf. Every nontrivial tree has at least two leaves. \(\square\)

**Lemma R4 (two-interface reflection and coverage).** Suppose two edges of \(T\) have the same target edge \(e\). The unique component between them after removing those two edges, including all its side branches, can be reflected by \(\sigma_e\). This cancels both interfaces and preserves all other join statuses. If \(C\) is its color set and \(O\) the outside color set, coverage is lost exactly when some color \(f\) satisfies

\[
f\in C\setminus O,\qquad \sigma_e(f)\notin C.
\]

**Proof.** The component has exactly the two removed interfaces as attachments to its complement. Pointwise edge fixing preserves their endpoint identifications; all other joins have both incident frames acted upon or neither. After reflection the occupied colors are \(O\cup\sigma_e(C)\). Since \(C\cup O\) is the full six-color set and \(\sigma_e\) is an involution, a missing color is exactly one described by the displayed condition. Global root normalization, when necessary, preserves coverage and statuses. \(\square\)

**Lemma R5 (no three equal target edges on a path).** No simple path of an active-arc-minimal tree \(T\) contains three edges with the same target edge.

**Proof.** Let the three occurrences, in path order, be \(e_1,e_2,e_3\), with incident colors \(A,B\). Let \(S_{12}\) and \(S_{23}\) be the full middle components between the first two and the last two occurrences. They are disjoint, including their side branches. The third occurrence lies entirely outside \(S_{12}\), supplying both \(A\) and \(B\) there; similarly the first occurrence supplies both outside \(S_{23}\).

Minimality forces each reflection in Lemma R4 to lose a color. That color cannot be \(A\), \(B\), or either fixed color. Thus each middle component contains every occurrence of one of \(A^*,B^*\) and no occurrence of the other. Disjoint components cannot both contain every occurrence of the same globally occupied color. They therefore confine different opposite colors. The larger middle component \(S_{13}\) contains both smaller ones and both endpoint regions of \(e_2\), hence all four moved colors \(A,B,A^*,B^*\). Reflecting \(S_{13}\) preserves all moved colors inside it and fixes the other two, preserving coverage while cancelling two active arcs. This contradicts minimality. \(\square\)

**Lemma R6 (twelve-arc bound).** The tree \(T\) has \(5\le a\le12\) active arcs.

**Proof.** Six occupied colors require at least six regions, hence \(a\ge5\). Suppress degree-two vertices of \(T\); let \(s\) be the number of resulting edges, equivalently the number of maximal chains. The degree formula for a tree gives at most \(\ell-2\) branch vertices and hence \(s\le2\ell-3\).

Fix a target edge \(e\) joining two nonleaf colors. Let \(q_e\) count its occurrences and \(r_e\) count chains containing it. Between consecutive occurrences on each chain are \(q_e-r_e\) middle intervals. These intervals have disjoint vertex sets, even across chains, because they exclude chain endpoints and lie in disjoint degree-two interiors. If \(q_e\ge3\), another entire occurrence lies outside each such interval. Both incident colors therefore occur outside. By Lemma R4 and minimality, every interval globally confines one of the opposite incident colors. It cannot confine a leaf color, since leaves are outside every chain interior. One globally occupied color cannot be confined in two disjoint intervals. If \(t_e\) is the number of opposite incident colors among the nonleaf colors, it follows that

\[
q_e-r_e\le t_e\quad(q_e\ge3),\qquad
q_e\le\max\{2,s+t_e\}.
\]

When \(\ell=2\), the tree is one chain and Lemma R5 gives the stronger \(q_e\le2\). There are four nonleaf colors. Their induced subgraph in the cube-face adjacency graph has at most five edges: every four faces contain at least one opposite pair. The two leaf edges and at most two occurrences of each nonleaf target edge give \(a\le2+2\cdot5=12\).

When \(\ell=3\), the three nonleaf colors form a connected subgraph. With no opposite pair they form a triangle, and every \(t_e=0\); since \(s\le3\), the bound is \(a\le3+3\cdot3=12\). With an opposite pair they form a two-edge path; each of its edges has \(t_e=1\), giving \(a\le3+2\cdot4=11\).

When \(\ell=4\), the two nonleaf colors must be adjacent. Only one nonleaf target edge is possible; its \(t_e=0\) and \(s\le5\), giving \(a\le4+5=9\). These cases exhaust Lemma R3. \(\square\)

**Lemma R7 (four leaves give six regions).** If \(T\) has four leaves, then \(a=5\), and each cube face has exactly one region.

**Proof.** The nonleaves induce a connected subtree \(H\) with exactly two colors \(A,B\), both occurring. They are adjacent, so every edge of \(H\) has the same target edge. Lemma R5 bounds its diameter by two; thus \(H\) is a star, including the single-edge case. Name its center color \(A\) and its arm colors \(B\). Suppose there are at least two arms.

Each arm vertex is a nonleaf of \(T\) whose only neighbor in \(H\) is the center, so it has a leaf of \(T\) attached. The exclusive leaf of color \(B^*\) can attach only to the \(A\) center, and the exclusive leaf of color \(A^*\) attaches to exactly one \(B\) arm. Choose a different arm and reflect that entire arm together with its leaf children through \(\sigma_{A\cap B}\). Neither opposite moved color lies in this component. Any of its leaf colors is one of the two fixed colors; outside, the center and another arm retain both \(A\) and \(B\). Coverage is preserved and one arc is cancelled. After root normalization when necessary, as in Lemma R3, the result lies in the family by Lemma R2, contradicting minimality. Thus \(H\) is a single edge. Together with the four exclusive leaves it gives six uniquely colored regions and five arcs. \(\square\)

For the remaining two- and three-leaf cases, the trees are respectively paths and Y-shaped trees. Indeed \(\sum_v(\deg(v)-2)=-2\); with three leaves there is exactly one degree-three vertex and all other nonleaves have degree two. Lemmas R3 and R6 therefore reduce the universal source problem to paths and Y-shaped trees with five through twelve edges, six face colors, adjacent consecutive colors, and globally exclusive leaf colors. These are necessary conditions only. Enumerating all trees satisfying them may include unrealizable source geometries, which is harmless for a universal reduction.

## 3. The complete finite certificate

Index the cube faces by \(0,\ldots,5\) with opposite pairs \((0,1)\), \((2,3)\), and \((4,5)\). A colored tree has a face color at each vertex, and the colors at the ends of every edge are adjacent, that is, distinct faces sharing a cube edge (Section 2). We also record the action of each symmetry on the eight cube vertices. This is a redundant, concrete representation: the 48 symmetries act faithfully on the faces.

**Lemma C1 (full-frame evaluation).** Let \(M\) be a member of a fixed toggle family, used as the base map, and let \(T\) be its contracted region tree, obtained from the original region tree by contracting the original arcs that are inactive in \(M\). The vertices of \(T\) are the regions of \(M\), with face colors \(c_v\), and its edges are the active original arcs of \(M\), which are exactly the whole bend arcs of \(M\) (see the paragraph after Lemma R2). Root \(T\), and let \(e=(p,v)\) be an edge of \(T\), with target cube edge \(E_e\) under \(M\). For a chosen bit vector \(b\) on the edges of \(T\), define cube isometries recursively by

\[
g_{\mathrm{root}}=\mathrm{id},\qquad
g_v=\begin{cases}
g_p,&\quad b_e=1,\\
g_p\circ\sigma_{E_e},&\quad b_e=0.
\end{cases}
\]

Apply \(g_v\) to the complete frames of \(M\) on every square in region \(v\). The result is a consistent map in which the joins along an edge \(e\) of \(T\) are 90-degree joins exactly when \(b_e=1\), and all other retained joins are 180-degree joins; its coverage is \(\{g_v(c_v):v\in V(T)\}\). After one global isometry restoring the fixed source square's frame, when necessary, it becomes the member of the same family whose active original arcs are exactly the edges \(e\) with \(b_e=1\), with the same number of covered faces.

**Proof.** On an interface endpoint \(x\in E_e\), either formula gives \(g_v(x)=g_p(x)\), since \(\sigma_{E_e}\) fixes both endpoints. The two incident face colors remain distinct for an active bit and become equal for an inactive bit. Internal joins of a region undergo one common isometry and remain consistent and inactive. The current-edge formulation of an actual sequence of toggles agrees with the recurrence because

\[
\sigma_{g(E)}=g\sigma_Eg^{-1},\qquad
\sigma_{g(E)}g=g\sigma_E.
\]

Thus ancestor transformations do not invalidate the use of the target edge \(E_e\) of \(M\) in the recurrence. No tangent-frame information is needed to identify that edge: the intersection of two adjacent cube faces is a unique edge. If the root region does not contain the fixed source square, apply one global isometry to restore that square's frame; this changes neither the number of covered faces nor any join status. Every original arc is then either an edge \(e\) of \(T\), active along its whole length exactly when \(b_e=1\), or an arc inactive in \(M\), whose joins lie inside one region of \(T\) and remain 180 degrees, as do all originally internal joins. The family member with these arc statuses therefore has the same root frame and the same join statuses, and uniqueness in Lemma R2 identifies the two maps. \(\square\)

The isometries \(g_v\) depend only on the rooted colored tree \((T,c)\) and on \(b\), since \(E_e\) is the common edge of the adjacent faces \(c_p\) and \(c_v\). For an abstract colored tree, the **full-frame evaluation** of a bit vector \(b\) is this recurrence together with the colors \(g_v(c_v)\). As in the proof, \(g_p(c_p)\) and \(g_v(c_v)\) differ exactly when \(b_e=1\); these edges are the active arcs of the evaluation. Because each \(\sigma_E\) is an involution, the rule \(g_v=g_p\sigma_{E_e}^{1-b_e}\) is equivalent to \(g_p=g_v\sigma_{E_e}^{1-b_e}\). Hence, for another root \(r'\), the recurrence gives \(g_{r'}^{-1}g_v\) in place of \(g_v\): a change of root moves all colors by one isometry and changes neither the number of covered faces nor any status.

### 3.3 Consequence for physical disks

**Proposition C3.** Every physical disk admitting a surjective consistent complete-frame map has a surjective member of the fixed toggle family of that map with at most seven active arcs; indeed every active-arc-minimal surjective member has at most seven, and a member's active original arcs are exactly its whole bend arcs.

**Proof.** Let \(M\) be an active-arc-minimal surjective member of the family; one exists because the family is finite and contains the given map. Let \(T\) be its contracted region tree, with face colors \(c\). Lemmas R3 and R6 give two through four leaves and five through twelve arcs. The four-leaf case has five arcs by Lemma R7. Otherwise \(T\) is a path or a Y tree; suppose that it has at least eight arcs.

By Section 3.1 there are a cube isometry \(h\) and an isomorphism \(\psi\) from \(T\) onto one of the enumerated trees (for a path, either orientation; for a Y tree, a suitable ordering of its arms) such that \(c'=h\circ c\circ\psi^{-1}\) has normalized leaf colors. Since \(h\) preserves face adjacency, six-face coverage and the exclusivity of leaf colors, \(c'\) is a domain input. By C2 it has a bit vector \(b'\) with at most seven active bits whose full-frame evaluation \(g'\) covers all six faces. Put \(b_e=b'_{\psi(e)}\), root \(T\) and \(\psi(T)\) at corresponding vertices (by the paragraph after Lemma C1, the root is immaterial), and let \(g\) be the full-frame evaluation of \(b\) on \((T,c)\). The target edges correspond, \(E'_{\psi(e)}=h(c_p)\cap h(c_v)=h(E_e)\), so the identity \(\sigma_{h(E)}=h\sigma_Eh^{-1}\) from the proof of Lemma C1 and induction from the root give \(g'_{\psi(v)}=hg_vh^{-1}\). Hence \(g'_{\psi(v)}(c'_{\psi(v)})=h(g_v(c_v))\). The evaluation of \(b\) therefore also covers all six faces, and \(b\) has as many active bits as \(b'\).

Lemma C1 with \(M\) as base map now gives a surjective member of the same family whose active original arcs are the at most seven edges \(e\) of \(T\) with \(b_e=1\), contradicting minimality. Thus every active-arc-minimal surjective member has at most seven active arcs. By the paragraph after Lemma R2, the active original arcs of any member are exactly its whole bend arcs. \(\square\)

This is the only exhaustive computational obligation in the main theorem. The argument reducing arbitrary physical disks to this domain is analytic, as is the downstream layer construction. The supplement documents its invocation, tested interpreter, data hashes, validation behavior, and fresh execution output. The finite certificate does not by itself establish any geometric or topological lemma.

## 4. Layer construction on the unchanged physical disk

This section proves the constructive layer-order step. Its conclusions concern the original cells and **all** original retained joins. The region decomposition used in the proof does not authorize a new slit. Whole cube isometries may change the prescribed map; an ordering reversal changes only layers.

### 4.1 Ports, orders, and the geometric interface

A cell with lower-left coordinate \((x,y)\) has ports \(0,1,2,3\), respectively south, east, north, west. Its corners are indexed counterclockwise as bottom-left, bottom-right, top-right, top-left. A complete frame sends these four corners bijectively to the four cyclically ordered vertices of a cube face. Across a retained join \((a,p;b,q)\), where \(q=p+2\pmod4\), the two endpoint images agree in reverse boundary order. Source corners are identified only through actual retained joins. Coincident coordinates on different slit banks or split corner fans are not an identification.

Write \(F(a)\) for the face occupied by cell \(a\). A retained join is a **flip** when \(F(a)=F(b)\), corresponding to a folded angle of \(180^\circ\), and a **bend** otherwise, corresponding to \(90^\circ\) between adjacent cube faces. A layer assignment gives a strict total order \(<_F\) to the cells on each face \(F\); equivalently, it gives distinct ranks \(0,\ldots,n_F-1\), increasing away from the cube.

**Lemma L1 (edge-order criterion).** Fix a target cube edge \(e\) between faces \(F,G\). As in Section 1.2, list the cells on \(F\) in increasing \(<_F\)-order followed by the cells on \(G\) in decreasing \(<_G\)-order around a circle, and represent each retained join at \(e\) by the chord between its two endpoint cells. No two of these chords have alternating endpoints exactly when the following conditions hold:

1. Two flips \(ab,cd\) on the same face do not alternate: neither \(a<c<b<d\) nor \(c<a<d<b\), after ordering the endpoints within each pair. Flips on different incident faces require no comparison.
2. For a flip \(ab\) on one incident face \(H\in\{F,G\}\) and a bend \(cd\) with \(c\) on \(H\), \(c\) is not strictly between \(a\) and \(b\) in \(<_H\).
3. For bends \(ab,cd\), with \(a,c\) on \(F\) and \(b,d\) on \(G\),
   \[
   a<_F c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b<_G d.
   \]

**Proof.** Two flips on one face have all four endpoints in that face's arc of the circle, where they alternate exactly as excluded in condition 1; reversing the order of an arc does not affect alternation. Flips on different faces have their endpoints in disjoint arcs and never alternate. For a flip \(ab\) on \(H\) and a bend \(cd\) with \(c\) on \(H\), the endpoint \(d\) lies outside the arc of \(H\), so the two chords alternate exactly when \(c\) is strictly between \(a\) and \(b\). For bends \(ab,cd\) as in condition 3 with \(a<_F c\), the cyclic order is \(a,c,d,b\) if \(b<_G d\) and \(a,c,b,d\) if \(d<_G b\); only the second alternates, and the case \(c<_F a\) is symmetric. Distinct joins at \(e\) have distinct endpoint cells: a complete frame has exactly one port on that edge, and a source port belongs to at most one retained join. Thus no shared-endpoint exception is suppressed. Conditions 1, 2 and 3 are the comparisons 3, 2 and 1 of Section 1.2, so orders satisfying them at every cube edge are exactly the locally feasible orders defined there. The lemma concerns orders only. For locally feasible orders, Lemma M1 realizes the joins at each cube edge by pairwise disjoint polygonal arcs in its outward transverse quadrant; the realization of the whole source is Theorem M3, and Corollary D1 makes it a valid folding onto the cube when the source is a disk and the map is surjective. \(\square\)

As noted in Section 1.2, conditions 1, 2 and 3 are the cube-edge analogues of the taco-taco, taco-tortilla and tortilla-tortilla constraints for flat folded states [ADK26], a setting without cube edges.

The following conclusions of Section 2 (Lemma R1 and its proof, Lemma R2, and the contraction paragraph after Lemma R2) are the geometric input here:

**Geometric interface (G).** In a consistently mapped physical disk, the maximal \(90^\circ\) crease runs are proper straight arcs with disjoint interiors, and every interior vertex of an arc is a full star. Decomposing along \(k\) such arcs yields \(k+1\) all-flip physical disks, the regions, whose interface graph is a tree. Several arcs may end at one boundary fan; their final joins split it into consecutive subfans, which lie in distinct regions. A finite, root-normalized toggle family changes whole original arcs between bends and flips, keeps every originally internal join a flip, preserves complete-frame consistency on the unchanged source, and contracts inactive edges of the original region tree. It contains the starting map. In each member, whose regions are those of the contracted tree, every interface is a whole active original arc, a straight run of retained joins. At an interior vertex of such an arc, the two cells on either side of the arc are joined along the tangent direction by an original flip (Lemma R1), which remains a flip in every member (Lemma R2). These two cells therefore form the whole fan of their region at that vertex, and the other two cells lie across the arc in the adjacent region. Hence each interface bank, formed by the interface sides of the cells of one incident region, is one interval of that region's boundary walk, and its consecutive parents are joined along the tangent direction.

All statements below use entire interface runs, not representatives of length one. An **all-flip region** is a retained-join-connected set of cells occupying one face. A **bank** is a nonempty interval of an actual boundary walk, with a fixed source port and consecutive, distinct parent cells joined along the tangent direction. Its target cube edge is fixed. Different banks select disjoint halfedges, although banks on different target edges may share a parent. A selected halfedge is **outward** relative to a region when the adjacent square coordinate is absent from that region. This is stronger than merely being unjoined there.

A layer order **exposes** a marked halfedge on target edge \(e\) if its parent is not strictly between the endpoints of any internal flip at \(e\). It is **monotone** on a bank if its parents increase or decrease in their straight tangent-coordinate order. The direction is chosen, not prescribed. Two banks are **separated** if every parent of one precedes every parent of the other. These properties are invariant under reversal of the whole region order.

### 4.2 Cofacial bank orders by straight cuts

We order the cells of one all-flip region by induction along straight runs of its joins. As in Section 2, these cuts are decompositions for the proof: every retained join of the region is kept and checked. The external inputs are standard PL topology: the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem [MT01, Section 2.2] and the \(\theta\)-graph lemma [MT01, Section 2.1], which give (Sch) below; and the classification of compact PL surfaces, which also underlies the recognition criterion of Section 1.1. (Sch) is a statement of PL topology alone; the proof of Lemma R1 uses it, together with the argument of Part 2 of Lemma L2.2.

**(Sch)** Let \(\Gamma\) be a proper PL arc with endpoints \(p\ne q\) in a closed PL disk \(D\). Then \(p,q\) divide \(\partial D\) into two arcs \(\gamma_1,\gamma_2\), the complement \(D\setminus \Gamma\) has exactly two components, and their closures \(D_1,D_2\) are closed disks with \(\partial D_i=\gamma_i\cup \Gamma\), \(D_i\cap\partial D=\gamma_i\), \(D_1\cup D_2=D\) and \(D_1\cap D_2=\Gamma\).

**Proof.** Identify \(D\) PL with a convex polygon; the interior of \(\Gamma\) lies in the interior of \(D\). The \(\theta\)-graph \(\Theta=\gamma_1\cup\gamma_2\cup\Gamma\) divides the plane into exactly three faces, each bounded by one of its three cycles [MT01, Section 2.1]. The set \(\mathbb R^2\setminus\Theta\) is the disjoint union of the open sets \(\mathbb R^2\setminus D\) and \(\mathrm{int}\,D\setminus\Gamma\), so each face, being connected, lies in one of them. Since \(D\) is convex, \(\mathbb R^2\setminus D\) is connected; it is unbounded and misses \(\Theta\), so it lies in the unbounded face, which is therefore \(\mathbb R^2\setminus D\). Its frontier is \(\partial D=\gamma_1\cup\gamma_2\). The other two faces \(U_1,U_2\), bounded by \(\gamma_1\cup\Gamma\) and \(\gamma_2\cup\Gamma\), are therefore the components of \(\mathrm{int}\,D\setminus\Gamma\). The frontier of \(U_i\) is the Jordan curve \(\gamma_i\cup\Gamma\), so the connected open set \(U_i\) is also closed in \(\mathbb R^2\setminus(\gamma_i\cup\Gamma)\); it is therefore a component of that set, and the bounded one. By the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem [MT01, Section 2.2], \(D_i=U_i\cup\gamma_i\cup\Gamma\) is a closed disk with \(\partial D_i=\gamma_i\cup\Gamma\). Since \(U_i\subset\mathrm{int}\,D\) and \(\Gamma\cap\partial D=\{p,q\}\subset\gamma_i\), also \(D_i\cap\partial D=\gamma_i\). Then \(D_1\cup D_2=D\), and \(D_1\cap D_2=\Gamma\) because \(U_1\cap U_2=\emptyset\) and \(\gamma_1\cap\gamma_2=\{p,q\}\). The sets \(D_i\setminus\Gamma=U_i\cup(\gamma_i\setminus\{p,q\})\) are connected, disjoint and relatively closed in \(D\setminus\Gamma\), so they are its two components. \(\square\)

Throughout, \(Q\) is an **all-flip physical disk**: a physical source in the sense of Section 1.1 that is homeomorphic to a closed disk, with all cells on one face. Such a source is a compact PL surface homeomorphic to a disk, so by the classification of compact PL surfaces it is a PL disk, and (Sch) applies to it. These are the regions supplied by (G) for whichever member of the toggle family is in use, equivalently the contracted monochrome disk regions of Section 2, including those left by the leaf reflections of L6: a contraction, or a leaf reflection in L6, glues two disks along an arc that lies in the boundary of each (the whole interface run), and the result is a disk. Distinct cells are distinct lattice squares, and corners are identified only through retained joins. Hence a connected physical source has a connected join graph, and the coordinate map \(\pi:Q\to\mathbb R^2\) is injective on every straight run of joins. A vertex occurrence (corner fan) is *interior*, or a *full star*, when its fan has four cells and all four joins (a circular link, as in Section 1.1; the full star of Section 2 and Lemma R1), and *boundary* otherwise; a boundary occurrence is *joint* when its fan has at least two cells. The counterclockwise boundary walk \(W=(W_0,\ldots,W_{L-1})\), with \(Q\) on the left, lists the boundary halfedges. A *position* \(t\in\mathbb Z/L\) is the boundary occurrence between \(W_{t-1}\) and \(W_t\), and these are its two *walk halfedges*. Boundary fans are intervals, so each boundary occurrence is exactly one position. Banks are intervals of \(W\), as in Section 4.1.

**Colors.** Fix a root cell \(c_\ast=(x_0,y_0)\) of \(Q\), with frame \(\beta_{c_\ast}(x,y)=o+xu+yv\), and let \(\tau:\mathbb R\to[0,1]\) be the distance to the nearest even integer. For a cell \(c=(x_c,y_c)\), the frames
\[
\beta_c(x,y)=o+\tau(x_c+x-x_0)\,u+\tau(y_c+y-y_0)\,v,\qquad 0\le x,y\le1,
\]
satisfy the join equations of Section 1.1 at every flip. Across a vertical side the two frames agree on the side and are mirror images in it, because \(\tau\) is even about every integer. So crossing the side reverses the target coordinate along \(u\) and fixes the one along \(v\); likewise across horizontal sides. A parent frame and a flip determine the child frame (proof of Lemma R2), and \(Q\) is join-connected, so these are the frames of \(Q\). Hence every side on a vertical lattice line \(X=\xi\), boundary sides included, maps onto the target edge on which the \(u\)-coordinate equals \(\tau(\xi-x_0)\in\{0,1\}\). This edge depends only on the parity of \(\xi\), and the two edges so obtained are opposite; call them the *vertical* target edges, and the two opposite edges met in the same way by horizontal lines the *horizontal* ones. Fix one of the two partitions \(\Pi\) of the target edges into adjacent pairs, and call its two classes, the *merged colors*, *red* and *blue*. Each class contains one vertical and one horizontal edge, so the colors of vertical lines alternate with parity, and so do those of horizontal lines. A join, side or port is colored by its target edge. A lattice point, and any position over it, is **pure red** (**pure blue**) when both lattice lines through it are red (blue), and **mixed** otherwise.

Two cyclically consecutive selected banks *meet* at a position \(t\) when the last halfedge of the first is \(W_{t-1}\) and the first halfedge of the second is \(W_t\).

**Lemma L2 (cofacial bank orders).** Let \(Q\) be an all-flip physical disk, and select banks on its boundary. Suppose that for one adjacent-pair partition \(\Pi\):

(H1) in the cyclic sequence of selected halfedges, listed in the order of \(W\), each merged color is consecutive (equivalently, the color changes at most twice);

(H2) both merged colors are selected, or some boundary halfedge is unselected, or two cyclically consecutive selected banks meet at a mixed position.

Then \(Q\) has a locally feasible order that exposes every selected halfedge, is monotone on every selected bank, and gives any two selected banks on the same target edge separated parent sets. No outwardness is assumed.

*Remarks.* (i) A walk traverses a bank in one tangent direction, and its consecutive parents are joined along the tangent. Monotonicity along \(W\) is therefore the tangent-coordinate monotonicity of Section 4.1. (ii) Condition (H2) fails exactly when every boundary halfedge is selected, all in one merged color, and every position at which two consecutive banks meet is pure. Even when every boundary halfedge is selected, all in one merged color, two consecutive banks may meet at a mixed position, for instance at the tip of a slit, where the walk turns back along the slit. For example, take the \(2\times4\) block of cells \((x,y)\) with \(0\le x\le1\) and \(0\le y\le3\), with the join between \((0,1)\) and \((0,2)\) slit, and select every boundary halfedge, the banks being the maximal intervals of the walk on which the port is constant. Every selected halfedge lies on one of the lines \(X=0,2\) and \(Y=0,2,4\), so all have one merged color for the partition that merges the two target edges of these lines. The banks formed by the south side of \((0,2)\) and the north side of \((0,1)\) meet at the tip \((1,2)\) of the slit, which is mixed, so (H2) holds. (H2) is sufficient; we do not claim that it is necessary. (iii) If the hypotheses hold for one partition, they hold for every partition satisfying (H1). Indeed, suppose \(\Pi\) satisfies (H1) but not (H2). Then every boundary halfedge is selected and lies on one of the two edges \(e_V,e_H\) of a single \(\Pi\)-class. Under the other partition, the color of a boundary halfedge is just its direction. Consecutive horizontal halfedges of \(W\) are collinear, and the north side of a topmost cell and the south side of a bottommost cell are on different horizontal lines. So the horizontal halfedges form at least two maximal runs along \(W\), the direction changes at least four times, and the other partition fails (H1). So a partition satisfying (H1) but not (H2) is the only partition satisfying (H1).

The proof occupies the rest of this subsection.

**Lemma L2.1 (parity).**

1. Every unit lattice segment has exactly one pure endpoint, and that endpoint has the segment's color.
2. A cell has one pure red and one pure blue corner, which are diagonally opposite, and two mixed corners. Its two sides at a pure corner have that corner's color. Its two sides at a mixed corner have different colors.
3. At a pure position, both walk halfedges have its color. At a non-joint position of a cell \(c\), the two walk halfedges are the two sides of \(c\) there.
4. Pure and mixed positions alternate along \(W\).
5. A cell has exactly one side on each target edge. Hence selected banks on one target edge have disjoint parent sets.

**Proof.** A segment on a line of color \(\lambda\) joins points on two consecutive perpendicular lines, one of each color. Its endpoint on the \(\lambda\)-colored line is pure of color \(\lambda\), and the other endpoint is mixed. This is 1. The corners of a cell lie on two consecutive vertical and two consecutive horizontal lines, so 1 gives 2. For 3, a halfedge at a pure point has that point as its pure endpoint; the second claim is the definition of a one-cell fan. Consecutive positions are the endpoints of one halfedge, so 1 gives 4. The two vertical sides of a cell lie on lines of different parity, and so do its two horizontal sides. Banks select disjoint halfedges, which gives 5. \(\square\)

A **cut** of \(Q\) is a maximal straight run of retained joins along one lattice line, all of whose interior vertices are interior occurrences. This is the continuation rule of Lemma R1, applied here to every join of an all-flip region.

**Lemma L2.2 (cuts split the disk).** Let \(\Gamma\) be a cut of \(Q\).

1. Every join lies on exactly one cut. The endpoints of \(\Gamma\) are distinct positions. All joins of \(\Gamma\) have one target edge and hence one color, and a pure endpoint of \(\Gamma\) has that color.
2. \(\Gamma\) divides \(Q\) into two all-flip physical disks \(Q_1,Q_2\), its *halves*. Identifying each \(Q_i\) with a closed subset of \(Q\) through the map induced by the inclusion of its cells, \(Q_1\cup Q_2=Q\) and \(Q_1\cap Q_2=\Gamma\). The two cells of each join of \(\Gamma\) lie in different \(Q_i\). Every other join has both cells in one \(Q_i\), and the joins of \(Q_i\) are exactly the joins of \(Q\) between its cells. In particular \(1\le|Q_i|<|Q|\).
3. The halfedges of \(W\) with parent in \(Q_i\) form a nonempty interval \(\alpha_i\) of \(W\), and \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\) are complementary. Name the endpoints of \(\Gamma\) so that \(W\) passes from \(\alpha_1\) to \(\alpha_2\) at \(p\) and from \(\alpha_2\) to \(\alpha_1\) at \(q\). The walk of \(Q_1\) is \(\alpha_1\), from \(q\) to \(p\), followed by the block \(\kappa_1\) of cut sides of its cells, from \(p\) to \(q\). The walk of \(Q_2\) is \(\alpha_2\), from \(p\) to \(q\), followed by \(\kappa_2\), from \(q\) to \(p\).
4. The parents of \(\kappa_1\), from \(p\) to \(q\), are distinct cells \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\), each \(a_i\) joined across \(\Gamma\) to \(b_i\in Q_2\). The parents of \(\kappa_2\), from \(q\) to \(p\), are \(b_k,\ldots,b_1\).

**Proof.** *Part 1.* Extend a join along its line in both directions. At an interior occurrence the full star contains the next collinear join; at a boundary occurrence the run stops. Since \(\pi\) is injective on the run, it cannot close up, and its two endpoints have different coordinates. A lattice line carries joins of only one target edge. A pure endpoint is the pure endpoint of the last join of \(\Gamma\), so it has that join's color by L2.1.

*Part 2.* The open sides of the joins of \(\Gamma\) and its interior vertices are interior points of \(Q\), while its endpoints lie on \(\partial Q\). So \(\Gamma\) is a proper arc. Apply (Sch) to obtain closed disks \(D_1,D_2\). An open cell misses \(\Gamma\), so each closed cell lies in one \(D_i\). Near a point of an open side not on \(\Gamma\), the source is a union of two half-disks, one in each adjacent cell, missing \(\Gamma\); so both cells of a join not on \(\Gamma\) lie in one \(D_i\). Near a point \(x\) of an open side on \(\Gamma\), the two half-disks are the two components of a neighborhood minus \(\Gamma\). Since \(x\in D_1\cap D_2\), both components of \(Q\setminus \Gamma\) meet this neighborhood, so the two cells lie in different \(D_i\).

Let \(Q_i\) be the physical source formed by the cells in \(D_i\) and the joins of \(Q\) among them; joins between two cells of \(D_i\) are not on \(\Gamma\). A fan of \(Q\) at a vertex off \(\Gamma\) contains no join of \(\Gamma\) and lies in one \(D_i\). At a vertex of \(\Gamma\) the fan contains one join of \(\Gamma\) (at an endpoint) or two opposite ones (at an interior vertex); removing them from the path or four-cycle leaves two subpaths, one in each \(D_i\). Since corners are identified only through joins, \(Q_i\) makes exactly the identifications that \(Q\) makes among points of \(D_i\). Every point of \(D_i\) lies in a closed cell of \(D_i\): a point of \(D_1\) in no \(D_1\)-cell would lie in a \(D_2\)-cell, hence in \(D_1\cap D_2=\Gamma\), which is covered by the cut sides of \(D_1\)-cells; likewise for \(D_2\). The induced map \(Q_i\to D_i\) is therefore a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space, hence a homeomorphism. So \(Q_i\) is an all-flip physical disk; it is connected, so its join graph is connected. It contains one cell of each join of \(\Gamma\), so \(1\le|Q_i|<|Q|\).

*Part 3.* By (Sch), \(D_1\cap\partial Q=\gamma_1\) is one of the two boundary arcs between \(p\) and \(q\). A halfedge with parent in \(Q_1\) lies in this arc. Conversely, a halfedge of \(\gamma_1\) with parent in \(Q_2\) would lie in \(D_1\cap D_2\cap\partial Q=\Gamma\cap\partial Q=\{p,q\}\), which is impossible. The counterclockwise walk of \(Q_1\) traverses \(\alpha_1\) with the same cells on the left as \(W\), hence in the same direction, and then traverses \(\Gamma\). The same holds for \(Q_2\). A position of \(W\) whose two walk halfedges lie in \(\alpha_i\) is a boundary occurrence other than \(p,q\), hence off \(\Gamma\), so by Part 2 it is a position of the walk of \(Q_i\) with the same fan. The fans at \(p\) and \(q\) split into one subfan in each half; these are the two positions at which the walk of \(Q_i\) passes between \(\alpha_i\) and \(\kappa_i\).

*Part 4.* Interior vertices of \(\Gamma\) are full stars, so consecutive \(a_i\) are joined, and \(\pi\) is injective on \(\Gamma\). \(\square\)

**Lemma L2.3 (cuts separate cells).** Any two distinct cells \(a,b\) of \(Q\) lie in different halves of some cut of \(Q\).

**Proof.** Induct on \(|Q|\). Since \(Q\) is join-connected, it has a join, and that join lies on a cut \(\Gamma\). If \(\Gamma\) separates \(a\) and \(b\) we are done; this is always the case when \(|Q|=2\), since both halves are then single cells by L2.2(2). Otherwise both lie in one half, which we name \(Q_1\), and \(Q\) has at least three cells. By induction, a cut \(\Gamma'\) of \(Q_1\) has \(a,b\) in different halves \(Q_a\ni a\) and \(Q_b\ni b\) of \(Q_1\), with \(Q_a\cap Q_b=\Gamma'\).

The joins of \(\Gamma'\) are joins of \(Q\) not on \(\Gamma\), and full stars of \(Q_1\) are full stars of \(Q\). Interior points of \(\Gamma'\) are interior points of \(Q_1\), while \(\Gamma\subset\partial Q_1\). So \(\Gamma'\) meets \(\Gamma\) only in endpoints of \(\Gamma'\). Let \(\Gamma''\) be the cut of \(Q\) containing \(\Gamma'\). It extends \(\Gamma'\) beyond an endpoint \(z\) exactly when \(z\) is an interior occurrence of \(Q\). Since \(z\in\partial Q_1=\gamma_1\cup \Gamma\), this happens only when \(z\) is an interior vertex of \(\Gamma\). In that case the star at \(z\) has two \(Q_1\)-cells in one half-plane of the lattice line containing \(\pi(\Gamma)\) and two \(Q_2\)-cells in the other, and the only join of \(Q_1\) at \(z\) is the one between its two cells there. So \(\Gamma'\) reaches \(z\) perpendicular to that line. Since \(\pi(\Gamma')\) is a straight segment, it meets that line in at most one point, and \(\pi\) is injective on \(\Gamma'\). So in that case \(\Gamma'\cap\Gamma=\{z\}\), and in particular at most one endpoint of \(\Gamma'\) is an interior vertex of \(\Gamma\).

*Case 1: \(\,\Gamma''=\Gamma'\).* Then \(\Gamma'\cap \Gamma\subset\{p,q\}\). The connected set \(\Gamma\setminus\{p,q\}\) lies in \(Q_1\setminus \Gamma'=(Q_a\setminus \Gamma')\sqcup(Q_b\setminus \Gamma')\), a disjoint union of relatively open sets. Hence it lies in one of them, say \(Q_a\setminus \Gamma'\), after exchanging the names \(a,b\) if necessary. A \(Q_1\)-cell along \(\Gamma\) has its open cut side in \(Q_a\setminus \Gamma'\), so it lies in \(Q_a\); otherwise its closed square would lie in \(Q_b\). Therefore every join of \(Q\) not on \(\Gamma''\) joins \(Q_a\) to \(Q_a\), \(Q_b\) to \(Q_b\), \(Q_2\) to \(Q_2\), or \(Q_a\) to \(Q_2\) (the joins of \(\Gamma\)). By L2.2 for \(\Gamma''\), each half of \(\Gamma''\) is a disk, hence join-connected, and only joins of \(\Gamma''\) connect the two halves. So the halves of \(\Gamma''\) are the join-components of \(Q\) after removing the joins of \(\Gamma''\). The set \(Q_b\) is closed under the remaining joins and misses \(a\). Hence \(a\) and \(b\) lie in different halves of \(\Gamma''\).

*Case 2: \(\,\Gamma'\) ends at an interior vertex \(z\) of \(\,\Gamma\).* Then \(\Gamma'\cap \Gamma=\{z\}\), as just shown, and \(\Gamma''=\Gamma'\cup \Gamma_2'\). Here \(\Gamma_2'\) continues straight from \(z\), through the join between the two \(Q_2\)-cells at \(z\), to an endpoint \(z_2\in\partial Q\).

The image \(\pi(\Gamma_2')\) meets the lattice line of \(\pi(\Gamma)\) only in \(\pi(z)\), and \(\pi\) is injective on \(\Gamma''\). So the connected set \(\Gamma_2'\setminus\{z\}\) misses \(\Gamma\). It contains the open side of the join between the two \(Q_2\)-cells at \(z\), a subset of \(Q_2\setminus \Gamma\), so it lies in \(Q_2\setminus \Gamma\). Therefore \(\Gamma_2'\) consists of joins of \(Q_2\), its interior vertices are full stars of \(Q_2\), and its endpoints \(z,z_2\) lie on \(\partial Q_2\): it is a cut of \(Q_2\).

The two \(Q_1\)-cells at \(z\) are the cells of the last join of \(\Gamma'\), so one lies in \(Q_a\) and the other in \(Q_b\). The point \(z\) divides \(\Gamma\) into subarcs \(\Gamma_a,\Gamma_b\), named so that the \(Q_a\)-cell at \(z\) has its cut side on \(\Gamma_a\) and the \(Q_b\)-cell on \(\Gamma_b\). The two \(Q_2\)-cells at \(z\) are the cells of the first join of \(\Gamma_2'\), so they lie in different halves of \(\Gamma_2'\). Let \(Q_{2,a}\) be the half containing the one with its cut side on \(\Gamma_a\), and \(Q_{2,b}\) the other half. The set \(\Gamma_a\setminus\{z\}\) is connected, misses \(\Gamma'\) and \(\Gamma_2'\), and contains the open cut sides of these two cells. As in Case 1, every \(Q_1\)-cell along \(\Gamma_a\) lies in \(Q_a\) and every \(Q_2\)-cell along \(\Gamma_a\) lies in \(Q_{2,a}\). Likewise the cells along \(\Gamma_b\) lie in \(Q_b\) and \(Q_{2,b}\).

Every join of \(Q\) outside \(\Gamma''\) therefore lies within \(Q_a\), \(Q_b\), \(Q_{2,a}\) or \(Q_{2,b}\), or joins \(Q_a\) to \(Q_{2,a}\) or \(Q_b\) to \(Q_{2,b}\). The set \(Q_b\cup Q_{2,b}\) is closed under these joins and misses \(a\). As in Case 1, \(a\) and \(b\) lie in different halves of \(\Gamma''\). \(\square\)

**Marked selections.** In Lemma L2.4 a *selection* \(\Sigma\) is an arbitrary set of boundary halfedges, and *markers* are two positions \(t_R,t_B\), with a *tie flag* when they coincide. The *red interval* \(I_R\) consists of the halfedges met walking forward from \(t_R\) to \(t_B\), and the *blue interval* \(I_B\) of the rest, from \(t_B\) to \(t_R\). For coincident markers the flag is their order: "\(t_R\) first" means that \(t_R\) precedes \(t_B\), so that \(I_R=\emptyset\), and "\(t_B\) first" means that \(t_B\) precedes \(t_R\), so that \(I_B=\emptyset\). The *color condition* requires every halfedge of \(\Sigma\cap I_R\) to be red and every halfedge of \(\Sigma\cap I_B\) to be blue. The *stretch* of a position is the maximal run of consecutive positions containing it that crosses no halfedge of \(\Sigma\), or all of \(W\) if \(\Sigma=\emptyset\). A marker is *admissible* when its stretch contains a mixed position. A *slide* moves a marker within its stretch without passing the other marker; it may land on the other marker, the flag then recording their previous order. A slide may separate coincident markers only in a way that keeps their order: with \(t_R\) first, only \(t_R\) moves backward or \(t_B\) forward; with \(t_B\) first, the reverse. Markers in different stretches impose no order constraint on each other. A slide does not change the sequences \(\Sigma\cap I_R\) and \(\Sigma\cap I_B\), so it preserves the color condition, admissibility and conclusion (S3) below. If \(\Sigma=\emptyset\), every placement of the markers satisfies the color condition and (S3) is vacuous; a slide is then any move, and the requirements below on the order of markers are void.

**Lemma L2.4 (marked orders).** Let \(\Sigma\) be a selection whose markers are admissible and satisfy the color condition. Then \(Q\) has a strict total order with the following properties.

(S1) The red joins are pairwise nonalternating, and so are the blue joins.

(S2) The parent of a red (blue) halfedge of \(\Sigma\) is not strictly between the endpoints of any red (blue) join.

(S3) Along \(I_R\), the parents of \(\Sigma\cap I_R\) are weakly increasing. Along \(I_B\), the parents of \(\Sigma\cap I_B\) are weakly decreasing.

**Proof.** Induct on \(|Q|\). A single cell has no joins and one parent. Let \(|Q|\ge2\). For a cut with halves and endpoints named as in L2.2, write \(\bar\alpha_1\) for the positions from \(q\) to \(p\) along \(\alpha_1\) and \(\bar\alpha_2\) for those from \(p\) to \(q\), with both ends included.

*Step 1: a separating cut.* We claim that, after slides, both markers are mixed and some cut \(\Gamma\) has \(t_R\in\bar\alpha_1\) and \(t_B\in\bar\alpha_2\). Moreover, coincident markers then lie at \(p\) with \(t_R\) first, or at \(q\) with \(t_B\) first.

The last requirement is a matter of naming. At an endpoint \(z\) of \(\Gamma\), the walk leaves one half's interval and enters the other's. Call the half left \(Q_1\) when \(t_R\) is first, so that \(z=p\), and the half entered \(Q_1\) when \(t_B\) is first, so that \(z=q\). Positions other than \(p,q\) lie in only one of \(\bar\alpha_1,\bar\alpha_2\).

Slide each marker to a mixed position of its stretch, keeping the order of markers that share a stretch.

*Case 1: the stretch of some marker contains a mixed joint position \(w\).* Slide the marker or markers whose stretch contains \(w\) to \(w\): first the one nearer to \(w\) along the stretch (for coincident markers, the one that may leave the tie toward \(w\)), then the other if it shares the stretch, which lands on the first with the flag recording their order. Two cells of the fan at \(w\) are joined along a side ending at \(w\), and the cut through this join ends at the boundary position \(w\). A cut endpoint lies in both \(\bar\alpha_i\), so the halves can be named as required.

*Case 2: neither stretch contains a mixed joint position.* Then each *available* mixed position \(x\), that is, each mixed position in the stretch of a marker, is a one-cell fan of a cell \(c(x)\), and both walk halfedges at \(x\) lie on \(c(x)\) (L2.1). Suppose the markers can be placed by slides at mixed positions \(x_R,x_B\) with \(c(x_R)\ne c(x_B)\). Then L2.3 gives a cut separating these cells. Naming the half of \(c(x_R)\) as \(Q_1\), we get \(t_R=x_R\) interior to \(\bar\alpha_1\) and \(t_B=x_B\) interior to \(\bar\alpha_2\).

Otherwise all available mixed positions are corners of one cell \(c\). Two distinct ones would be the two mixed corners of \(c\), both non-joint, and all four sides of \(c\) would be unjoined, which is impossible for \(|Q|\ge2\). So both markers lie in the stretch \(\Lambda\) of a single mixed position \(w\), and \(w\) is the only mixed position of \(\Lambda\).

- If \(\Sigma=\emptyset\), then \(\Lambda=W\), and every halfedge has \(w\) as its mixed endpoint. So \(W\) consists of the two walk halfedges at \(w\). These are perpendicular sides of \(c\) and do not close up, a contradiction.
- If \(\Sigma\ne\emptyset\), let \(\omega_-,\omega_+\) be the selected halfedges immediately before and after \(\Lambda\). By L2.1, \(\Lambda\) consists of \(w\) and possibly a pure neighbor at either end. If \(\Lambda\) begins at \(w\), then \(\omega_-=W_{w-1}\). If \(\Lambda\) begins at a pure position \(y\), then \(\omega_-\) and \(W_{w-1}\) are both walk halfedges at \(y\) and have its color. So \(\omega_-\) has the color of \(W_{w-1}\), and likewise \(\omega_+\) has the color of \(W_w\). These are the two sides of \(c\) at \(w\), which have different colors. So \(\Sigma\) contains both colors. But the markers coincide in the \(\Sigma\)-free stretch \(\Lambda\), so one of \(I_R,I_B\) contains all of \(\Sigma\), and the color condition makes \(\Sigma\) one color. This is a contradiction.

*Step 2: intervals.* For \(t\in\bar\alpha_1\), let \(\alpha_1^{<t}\) and \(\alpha_1^{>t}\) be the halfedges of \(\alpha_1\) from \(q\) to \(t\) and from \(t\) to \(p\). For \(t\in\bar\alpha_2\), let \(\alpha_2^{<t}\) and \(\alpha_2^{>t}\) be those of \(\alpha_2\) from \(p\) to \(t\) and from \(t\) to \(q\). Then
\[
I_R=\alpha_1^{>t_R}\,\alpha_2^{<t_B},\qquad I_B=\alpha_2^{>t_B}\,\alpha_1^{<t_R}.
\]
Walking forward from \(t_R\), the walk traverses \(\alpha_1^{>t_R}\) to \(p\), meeting no position of \(\bar\alpha_2\) other than \(p\) and its starting point (when \(t_R=q\)), and then traverses \(\alpha_2\) up to \(t_B\). The two ties are read off directly. With both markers at \(p\) and \(t_R\) first, \(I_R=\emptyset=\alpha_1^{>p}\alpha_2^{<p}\). With both at \(q\) and \(t_B\) first, \(I_R=W=\alpha_1^{>q}\alpha_2^{<q}\). In every case \(I_B\) is the complement of \(I_R\).

*Step 3: subproblems.* Put \(\Sigma_i=(\Sigma\cap\alpha_i)\cup\kappa_i\). In the walk of \(Q_1\), let \(p_1\) be the position between \(\alpha_1\) and \(\kappa_1\), and \(q_1\) the position between \(\kappa_1\) and \(\alpha_1\). In the walk of \(Q_2\), let \(q_2\) be the position between \(\alpha_2\) and \(\kappa_2\), and \(p_2\) the position between \(\kappa_2\) and \(\alpha_2\). The subproblem \(Q_1\) inherits \(t_R\), and \(Q_2\) inherits \(t_B\); write \(t_R^1=t_R\) and \(t_B^2=t_B\) for the inherited markers, so that subproblem \(i\) has markers \(t_R^i,t_B^i\). A marker at \(p\) or \(q\) becomes \(p_i\) or \(q_i\), and other positions are unchanged. The new markers are \(t_B^1=q_1\) and \(t_R^2=q_2\), both with "\(t_B\) first", for a red cut, and \(t_B^1=p_1\) and \(t_R^2=p_2\), both with "\(t_R\) first", for a blue cut.

Reading the walks \(\alpha_i\kappa_i\) gives, for every position of the inherited markers:
\[
\text{red cut: } I^1_R=\alpha_1^{>t_R}\kappa_1,\quad I^1_B=\alpha_1^{<t_R},\quad I^2_R=\kappa_2\alpha_2^{<t_B},\quad I^2_B=\alpha_2^{>t_B};
\]
\[
\text{blue cut: } I^1_R=\alpha_1^{>t_R},\quad I^1_B=\kappa_1\alpha_1^{<t_R},\quad I^2_R=\alpha_2^{<t_B},\quad I^2_B=\alpha_2^{>t_B}\kappa_2.
\]
Before the slides below, coincident markers occur in a subproblem exactly when the cut is red and \(t_R=q\) or \(t_B=q\), or the cut is blue and \(t_R=p\) or \(t_B=p\). The flags were chosen to give these formulas in those cases. With Step 2, the formulas give
\[
I_R=(I^1_R\setminus\kappa_1)(I^2_R\setminus\kappa_2),\qquad I_B=(I^2_B\setminus\kappa_2)(I^1_B\setminus\kappa_1).
\]

New markers at a pure position are moved. For a red cut with \(q\) pure, move \(t_B^1\) forward from \(q_1\) over the first halfedge \(h^+\) of \(\alpha_1\), and \(t_R^2\) backward from \(q_2\) over the last halfedge \(h^-\) of \(\alpha_2\). For a blue cut with \(p\) pure, move \(t_B^1\) backward from \(p_1\) over the last halfedge \(g^-\) of \(\alpha_1\), and \(t_R^2\) forward from \(p_2\) over the first halfedge \(g^+\) of \(\alpha_2\).

These moves are slides. Take a red cut with \(q\) pure. Then \(q\) is pure red (L2.2), so \(h^\pm\) are red (L2.1). These halfedges lie in \(\alpha_1\) or \(\alpha_2\), so they are in \(\Sigma_i\) only if they are in \(\Sigma\). If \(h^+\in\Sigma\), the color condition puts it in \(I_R\cap\alpha_1=\alpha_1^{>t_R}\). Since \(h^+\) is the first halfedge of \(\alpha_1\), this forces \(t_R=q\). If \(h^-\in\Sigma\), then \(h^-\in I_R\cap\alpha_2=\alpha_2^{<t_B}\), forcing \(t_B=q\). For a blue cut with \(p\) pure, \(g^-\in\Sigma\) would give \(g^-\in\alpha_1^{<t_R}\) and force \(t_R=p\), and \(g^+\in\Sigma\) would give \(g^+\in\alpha_2^{>t_B}\) and force \(t_B=p\). In each case the forced marker sits at a pure position, contrary to Step 1; so none of \(h^\pm,g^\pm\) is selected.

Each move takes one step onto a mixed position (L2.1). The other marker of the subproblem is inherited and hence mixed, so the move passes nothing. If the moved marker lands on the other one, the order before the move is the assigned flag: \(t_B^i\) precedes \(t_R^i\) for a red cut, and \(t_R^i\) precedes \(t_B^i\) for a blue cut. These landings are the only other coincidences, and their flags are again "\(t_B\) first" for a red cut and "\(t_R\) first" for a blue cut. So the selected parts of the four intervals are still those of the displayed formulas. All four markers are now mixed and hence admissible. The color condition holds in both subproblems: for a red cut, \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_1^{>t_R}\subset\Sigma\cap I_R\) and \(\kappa_1\) are red and \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_1^{<t_R}\subset\Sigma\cap I_B\) is blue, and \(Q_2\) is the same; a blue cut is symmetric, with \(\kappa_i\) blue. By induction, \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) have orders satisfying (S1)–(S3).

*Step 4: combination.* Let \(O\) list the order of \(Q_1\) followed by the order of \(Q_2\). Use the notation of L2.2.

*Red cut.*

- *Order of the cut parents.* In \(Q_1\), \(\kappa_1\) is the final part of \(\Sigma_1\cap I_R^1\) in walk order, so (S3) and distinctness give \(a_1<\cdots<a_k\). A red halfedge of \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_1\) lies in \(\alpha_1^{>t_R}\), before \(\kappa_1\), so its parent \(\mu\) satisfies \(\mu\le a_1\). In \(Q_2\), \(\kappa_2\) is the initial part of \(\Sigma_2\cap I_R^2\). So \(b_k<\cdots<b_1\), and every red parent \(\mu\) of \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_2\) satisfies \(\mu\ge b_1\).
- *(S1).* Joins inside one block are handled by induction, and joins in different blocks occupy disjoint intervals of \(O\). Since \(a_1<\cdots<a_k<b_k<\cdots<b_1\), the cut joins are nested. A red join \(xy\) of \(Q_1\) alternates with \(a_ib_i\) only if \(x<a_i<y\). This is excluded by (S2) in \(Q_1\), because the cut side of \(a_i\) is a red halfedge of \(\Sigma_1\). The case of \(Q_2\) is symmetric. No blue join meets both blocks.
- *(S2).* Against joins of its own block, a parent is protected by induction. Against joins of the other block, it is protected by disjointness of the blocks. Against the cut joins, only red parents need checking. A red parent \(\mu\in Q_1\) is strictly inside \(a_ib_i\) only if \(a_i<\mu\), which contradicts \(\mu\le a_1\). A red parent \(\mu\in Q_2\) is strictly inside only if \(\mu<b_i\), which contradicts \(\mu\ge b_1\).

*Blue cut.* Now \(\kappa_1\) is the initial part of \(\Sigma_1\cap I_B^1\) and \(\kappa_2\) the final part of \(\Sigma_2\cap I_B^2\). This gives \(a_k<\cdots<a_1<b_1<\cdots<b_k\). Every blue parent \(\mu\) of \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_1\) satisfies \(\mu\le a_k\), and every blue parent of \(\Sigma\cap\alpha_2\) satisfies \(\mu\ge b_k\). The same three arguments apply.

*(S3), both cuts.* By Step 2 and the subproblem formulas, the parents of \(\Sigma\cap I_R\), in walk order, are those of \(\Sigma_1\cap I^1_R\cap\alpha_1\), which are weakly increasing in the order of \(Q_1\), followed by those of \(\Sigma_2\cap I^2_R\cap\alpha_2\), which are weakly increasing and larger. The parents of \(\Sigma\cap I_B\) are those of \(\Sigma_2\cap I^2_B\cap\alpha_2\), weakly decreasing, followed by those of \(\Sigma_1\cap I^1_B\cap\alpha_1\), weakly decreasing and smaller. The slides of Step 1 do not affect (S3). No block is ever reversed: the marker conventions orient each block. \(\square\)

**Proof of Lemma L2.** A *gap* is the run of positions between two cyclically consecutive selected halfedges. A gap is *internal* when those halfedges are consecutive halfedges of one bank; it is then a single position. Choose the markers as follows.

- *Both colors are selected.* By (H1), exactly two gaps change color. Put \(t_R\) anywhere in the blue-to-red gap and \(t_B\) anywhere in the red-to-blue gap. A single-position gap between halfedges of different colors is not pure, by L2.1. A gap containing a halfedge contains that halfedge's mixed endpoint. So both markers are admissible. Banks are monochromatic, so neither gap is internal.
- *One color is selected.* By (H2), either some boundary halfedge is unselected, and its gap is not internal and contains its mixed endpoint, or two consecutive banks meet at a mixed position, which is a non-internal gap. Put both markers at that mixed position, with the flag that empties the interval of the absent color.
- *Nothing is selected.* Put both markers at any mixed position, with either tie flag.

The color condition holds, so Lemma L2.4 applies.

- *Local feasibility and exposure.* Joins and selected halfedges on one target edge share its color. So (S1) and (S2) give pairwise nonalternating flips and exposure on each target edge.
- *Monotonicity.* No marker lies in an internal gap. Hence each bank is a contiguous piece of the interval of its color. By (S3) its parents are weakly monotone along \(W\). They are distinct, so they are strictly monotone.
- *Separation.* Two banks on one target edge are disjoint pieces of the same interval. By (S3), every parent of the earlier piece is at most (along \(I_R\)) or at least (along \(I_B\)) every parent of the later piece. Their parent sets are disjoint by L2.1, so the banks are separated. \(\square\)

**Corollary L2a (configurations used below).** The hypotheses of Lemma L2 hold, without outwardness, in each of the following cases:

(a\) at most one bank on each target edge;

(b1) at most four banks, with at most one target edge repeated, and that edge at most twice;

(b2) any number of banks on one target edge and at most one other bank.

These cover every use in Sections 4.3–4.7. Corollary L3a and the remaining regions of L5–L7 are case (a). The common neighbor \(B\) in L5 is case (b1). The region \(F\) with \(m_F\ge2\) in L6, and every region of L7 with two banks on one target edge, are case (b2).

**Proof.** Each bank is a monochromatic block of the cyclic sequence of selected halfedges.

*(H1).* (a) With at most three banks, any two-coloring of at most three cyclic blocks changes color at most twice. With four banks on distinct edges \(e_1e_2e_3e_4\), in cyclic order, the three pairings of the four edges are \(\{e_1e_2\mid e_3e_4\}\), \(\{e_2e_3\mid e_4e_1\}\) and \(\{e_1e_3\mid e_2e_4\}\). Only the last alternates. The two adjacent-pair partitions are distinct pairings, so one of them satisfies (H1).

(b1) If no edge is repeated, this is case (a). Otherwise let \(e\) be the repeated edge. With at most one further bank there are at most three blocks. With two further banks, on distinct edges \(e',e''\ne e\), one of them, say \(e'\), is adjacent to \(e\). Merge \(e\) with \(e'\). The other class then contains only the block of \(e''\), and both classes are consecutive.

(b2) All banks on the main edge share a color, and the other bank is a single block.

*(H2).* Suppose (H2) fails for the chosen partition. Then every boundary halfedge is selected, and all lie on the two edges \(e_V,e_H\) of one class. The north side of a topmost cell and the south side of a bottommost cell are then selected on \(e_H\), and they lie in different banks, because a bank has one port. Likewise the west side of a leftmost cell and the east side of a rightmost cell lie in different banks on \(e_V\). So two target edges each carry at least two banks, which all three cases exclude. \(\square\)

**Remark L2b (necessity of (H2)).** (H2) cannot be omitted, even for outward banks. Let \(Q\) be the \(2m\times2n\) rectangle of cells filling \([0,2m]\times[0,2n]\), with all joins, where \(m,n\ge1\). Select its four whole sides as banks; all selected halfedges are outward. The bottom and top sides lie on the lines \(Y=0,2n\) of equal parity, hence on one target edge, and likewise the left and right sides. The partition merging these two edges makes every selected halfedge one color, so (H1) holds. (H2) fails: every halfedge is selected, and the four positions at which consecutive banks meet are the corners \((0,0),(2m,0),(2m,2n),(0,2n)\), all pure.

No order separates both same-edge pairs. Let \(a,b,c,d\) be the corner cells at the top left, top right, bottom right and bottom left. The reflections of the rectangle preserve the two pairs of opposite sides and permute the corners transitively, so we may assume that \(a\) is least among them. Separating the top from the bottom with \(a<c\) puts the top bank first, so \(b<d\). Separating the left from the right with \(a<b\) puts the left bank first, so \(d<b\), a contradiction. For the \(2\times2\) block, an exhaustive check of all 24 orders finds exactly eight that are locally feasible, expose every selected halfedge and are monotone on all four banks; only separation fails. The same check gives no valid orders and again eight satisfying the first three conclusions among the 40,320 orders of each of the \(4\times2\) and \(2\times4\) blocks (script `supplement/l2-bank-orders/check_counterexample.py`). Thus outwardness and consecutive merged colors do not by themselves give separation.

### 4.3 Reattaching every interface

**Lemma L3 (tree alignment and block assembly).** Suppose (G) gives a tree of all-flip regions, each equipped with a locally feasible order exposing all interface banks and monotone on them. The region orders can be reversed so that corresponding parents along every interface have the same relative order. After this alignment, concatenate region orders as whole blocks on each target face. The resulting layers satisfy all flip/flip and flip/bend constraints. Bend/bend constraints within an interface also hold. The only remaining constraints compare distinct interfaces at the same target cube edge.

**Proof.** Root the tree. Retain the root order and process outward. The parent's bank and the child's bank each order the same finite list of unit joins either forwards or backwards. One whole reversal of the child makes those orders agree; for a one-join interface no reversal is required. Each nonroot region has only one already oriented neighbor. Thus correlated directions of its other banks cause no obstruction, regardless of interface length.

A reversal preserves every strict-between and nonalternation statement. Under subsequent block concatenation, two internal flip intervals belonging to different same-face regions are disjoint. A bend endpoint outside a flip's region lies outside that entire block; an endpoint inside it is protected by exposure. Finally, adding rank offsets preserves the aligned comparisons within an interface. These are exactly the cases asserted in L1. Every original unit join is reattached and checked; no physical cut is made. \(\square\)

**Corollary L3a (distinct target edges).** A six-face disk map whose whole bend arcs use pairwise distinct target cube edges has locally feasible layers without changing that map.

**Proof.** Each region has at most one bank on each target edge, so L2 applies by Corollary L2a(a). Use L3 and choose arbitrary whole-region block orders on repeated faces. No target edge has two distinct bend interfaces, leaving no further comparisons. In particular, five active arcs give six regions on six distinct faces and satisfy this hypothesis. \(\square\)

### 4.4 Minimal maps and cancellation

Choose a six-face member of the finite toggle family minimizing its active original arcs, and let \(T\) be its contracted region tree. A face color of a leaf is **exclusive**: it occurs at no other vertex of \(T\).

**Lemma L4 (leaf exclusivity and three-edge cancellation).** Every leaf color is exclusive. Moreover, no simple path in \(T\) contains three active interfaces with the same target cube edge.

**Proof.** Leaf exclusivity is Lemma R3 of Section 2, and the arbitrary-path cancellation statement is Lemma R5 there. Those proofs act on complete physical region components, preserve every retained endpoint identification, and return to the same root-normalized family; no layer-order conclusion is assumed. \(\square\)

### 4.5 One repeated face and six arcs

**Proposition L5 (six-arc repair).** A physical disk admitting a six-face map with at most six whole bend arcs admits locally feasible layers for a six-face (surjective) member of that map's toggle family, on the unchanged source.

**Proof.** Minimize active arcs among the six-face members of its family, as in Section 4.4. There are \(k+1\) regions and \(5\le k\le6\). The case \(k=5\) follows from L3a. For \(k=6\), only one face \(A\) repeats, at regions \(A_1,A_2\). A target edge repeats only when these have a common neighbor \(B\). Two common neighbors would create a tree cycle. If none exists, apply L3a.

Both \(A_i\) are nonleaves by L4, so each has an edge away from \(B\). These are distinct edges not incident to \(B\), and consequently \(\deg(B)\le4\). Its banks consist of two on edge \(AB\) and at most two others, with distinct other colors. By Corollary L2a(b1), L2 orders \(B\), including separation of its two \(AB\)-banks. All other regions have at most one bank on each target edge and use L2 by L2a(a). Align via L3, then place the two \(A\)-blocks in the order of their parent-bank sets in \(B\). This handles the only comparison between distinct bend interfaces; L3 handles everything else. \(\square\)

**Proposition L6 (one repeated face, arbitrary multiplicity).** If a six-face disk map has at most one face represented by multiple all-flip regions, the same source admits a six-face (surjective) member of the given map's toggle family with locally feasible layers.

**Proof.** A leaf region on the repeated face can be reflected into its parent's face by the leaf reflection in the proof of Lemma R3, followed, when the root square lies in the leaf, by the global root normalization described there. By Lemma R2 the result is again a member of the given map's family. The leaf merges into its parent region, and the repeated face still occurs elsewhere, so coverage is preserved. The reflection lowers the multiplicity of the repeated face by one; the normalization may rename faces, but it preserves the multiplicity profile. So at most one face remains repeated. Continue until there is no repetition, or every remaining region of the repeated face is a nonleaf. If no repetition remains, the six regions lie on distinct faces, the map has five active arcs, and Corollary L3a applies. Otherwise let \(A\) be the repeated face, \(r\ge2\) the number of its regions, and \(T\) the region tree of the current map. Then \(T\) has \(r+5\) vertices and \(r+4\) edges. The degree sum of the \(A\)-regions is at least \(2r\) and counts distinct edges. The unique region on the face opposite \(A\) has an incident edge not counted there. Thus \(2r\le r+3\), or \(r\le3\). Multiplicity two is L5, applied to the current map; since toggling an original arc of a member gives another member (Lemma R2), the member it produces lies in the given map's family.

For \(r=3\), let a unique-face region \(F\) have \(m_F\) neighbors among \(A_1,A_2,A_3\). At least \(6-m_F\) distinct \(A\)-incident edges avoid \(F\), giving \(\deg(F)\le7-(6-m_F)=m_F+1\). When \(m_F\ge2\), its banks therefore consist of \(m_F\) banks on \(AF\) and at most one other. L2 applies by Corollary L2a(b2). All remaining regions have at most one bank on each target edge and use L2 by L2a(a).

After alignment, a common neighbor of several \(A_i\) imposes the order of its separated banks on their whole blocks. If one neighbor meets all three, no other can meet two, because two paths between that pair would create a cycle. Otherwise the pair-comparison graph on the three \(A_i\) is a forest: a repeated pair gives a four-cycle in \(T\), and a comparison triangle gives a six-cycle. Any orientation of a forest extends to a total order. Choose that block order on \(A\). Every comparison between distinct interfaces at \(AF\) agrees with \(F\)'s separated bank order, and L3 completes the proof. \(\square\)

### 4.6 Seven arcs

**Theorem L7 (seven-arc layer construction).** A physical disk admitting a consistent six-face map with at most seven whole bend arcs admits locally feasible layers for a six-face map on exactly the same physical source.

**Proof.** Minimize active arcs among the six-face members of the given map's toggle family, as in Section 4.4. L5 handles at most six. At seven, eight regions occupy six colors. Multiplicities \((3,1,1,1,1,1)\) are L6; consider \((2,2,1,1,1,1)\), with repeated regions \(A_1,A_2,B_1,B_2\). All four are nonleaves by L4. The faces \(A,B\) are adjacent: if opposite, no edge joins two repeated regions, and their degree sum at least eight would require eight distinct edges in a seven-edge tree.

Let \(H\) be the forest induced by these four vertices, with \(x\) edges, and let \(y\) count edges between unique-color vertices. Then
\[
\sum_{v\in H}\deg_T(v)=7+x-y\ge8.
\]
Thus \(x\ge1\). As a subgraph of \(K_{2,2}\), a three-edge \(H\) would be an alternating path with three occurrences of edge \(AB\), forbidden by L4. Hence \(x=1\) or \(2\). The four degrees then sum to \(7+x-y\le9\), and each is at least two, so every repeated vertex has degree at most three.

Such a vertex can have two banks on one target edge only through its two neighbors of the other repeated color. Now consider a unique-face vertex \(F\) with two banks on one target edge, say it meets \(A_1,A_2\). It cannot meet both \(B_i\), since any edge of \(H\) would then form a triangle through \(F\). Let \(m=2\) or \(3\) be its number of repeated neighbors. The repeated vertices have at least \(8-m-x\) distinct incident edges avoiding \(F\), so \(\deg(F)\le m+x-1\). For \(m=2\) this is at most three. For \(m=3\), every edge of \(H\) must use the fourth repeated vertex, or form a triangle. Two such edges would form a four-cycle through \(F\); therefore \(x=1\), again giving degree at most three.

Every region with two banks on one target edge thus has at most one other bank. Each region is a physical disk whose interfaces lie on its one boundary component. L2 supplies local orders, exposures, monotonicity and repeated-bank separation, by Corollary L2a(b2) for these regions and L2a(a) for the others. Use L3 to orient them. It remains to choose the two blocks on face \(A\) and the two on face \(B\).

The two regions on either repeated face have at most one common neighbor. Its separated banks, if present, force that face's block order. These include common neighbors on the other repeated face. The additional comparisons at \(AB\) have three cases:

- If \(H\) has one edge, it uses one block on each side and gives no comparison involving the other blocks. Choose each face order to satisfy its possible common-neighbor force.
- If \(H\) is the path \(A_1B_1A_2\), the separated banks in \(B_1\) fix the \(A\)-order. A second force on that order would create a tree cycle. Both interfaces lie in the one \(B_1\)-block, so placing \(B_2\) on either side adds no \(AB\)-comparison. Place it to satisfy any force on the \(B\)-order. The symmetric case is identical.
- If \(H\) is the matching \(A_1B_1,A_2B_2\), L1 requires the order of the \(A\)-blocks to equal the order of the corresponding \(B\)-blocks. Both cannot also be independently forced: common neighbors \(F\) of the \(A_i\) and \(G\) of the \(B_i\), together with the matching, form a cycle; if \(F=G\), a matching edge already gives a triangle. Neither common neighbor can be repeated without adding an edge to \(H\). At most one order is forced, so choose the other to agree.

Concatenate these blocks and assign dense ranks. L3 already proves the flip comparisons and within-interface bend comparisons. Between distinct interfaces at a unique face, its separated bank sets give precisely the common-neighbor constraint used above. Between \(A\) and \(B\), the three cases settle all pairs. No other cube edge has multiple interfaces. Therefore the conditions of L1 hold for all original retained joins at every cube edge. \(\square\)

### 4.7 The four-leaf reduction and realization boundary

**Proposition L8 (four leaves force five active arcs).** If an active-arc-minimal six-face family member has four region-tree leaves, it has exactly six regions on distinct faces and admits locally feasible layers preserving that member's complete map.

**Proof.** Lemma R7 of Section 2 gives exactly six regions, one on each cube face, and five active arcs. Corollary L3a supplies locally feasible layers for that same complete map. \(\square\)

L7 is a local-order construction for unbounded cell counts and interface lengths. Its conclusion is combinatorial: orders satisfying the conditions of L1 at every cube edge. Theorem M3 turns such orders into a PL embedding arbitrarily close to the map, and Corollary D1, which uses that the source is a disk, makes it a valid folding onto the cube. Together they give the corresponding seven-arc folding theorem: a physical disk admitting a consistent six-face map with at most seven whole bend arcs folds validly onto the cube. The decomposition interfaces of this section are all reattached before realization. The construction neither changes the prescribed physical source nor assumes that arbitrary preassigned bank directions are attainable.

## 5. Proof of the main theorem and its corollaries

**Proof of Theorem A.** A valid folding of \(P\) onto the cube corresponds to a surjective folding blueprint \(\beta:P\to C\) [ALL25, Definition 3.3]. By Lemma B1(c), \(\beta\) is a consistent complete-frame map: it carries each square onto one face of \(C\) by an affine isometry, and the frames of two joined squares agree on their common side because \(\beta\) is defined on the quotient \(P\). Surjectivity means that all six faces are covered. This proves necessity.

Conversely, start with a surjective consistent map of the physical disk \(P\). By Proposition C3, the fixed toggle family of this map contains a surjective member with at most seven active arcs, and a member's active original arcs are exactly its whole bend arcs. Theorem L7 therefore applies to this member and gives locally feasible total orders of the square layers for a six-face map on the same physical source, which may differ from the member. That construction preserves all source squares, retained joins, and actual corner identifications. Theorem M3 gives an embedded PL realization arbitrarily pointwise close to the resulting blueprint. Its image is a disk, so its boundary is an unknot. Corollary D1, using the validity criterion [ALL25, Theorem 3.4], makes this a valid folding onto the cube. \(\square\)

**Corollary A1 (Question 4.9 for disks).** Read the polyominoes and consistent mappings of [ALL25] as in Lemma B1. Every simply connected polyomino that admits a surjective consistent mapping to \(C\) admits a valid folding onto the cube. Hence a polyomino that admits surjective pseudo-foldings but no valid one, as asked for in [ALL25, Question 4.9], must have a hole.

**Proof.** Let \(P\) be such a polyomino. By Lemma B1(a) and (b), \(P\) is a physical disk, and by Lemma B1(c) its surjective consistent mapping is induced by a consistent complete-frame map covering all six faces. Theorem A gives a valid folding of \(P\) onto the cube. For the second assertion, let a simply connected polyomino admit a surjective pseudo-folding. Its consistent mapping is surjective, so the first assertion gives a valid folding onto the cube, and this folding defines a valid surjective pseudo-folding [ALL25, Section 4.1]. \(\square\)

The proof permits changing the original map and all layer orders. No claim is made that every surjective consistent map, or every surjective member of a toggle family, is realizable. Nor is a bounded-area enumeration used: the universal reduction bounds the number of whole active arcs, while region areas and arc lengths remain unrestricted.

The bound of Proposition C3 does, however, give a polynomial-time decision procedure, because the candidate arcs, the cuts defined in the proof below, depend only on the source.

**Corollary A2 (deciding foldability of disks).** Read the polyominoes of [ALL25] as in Lemma B1, and let a polyomino \(P\) with \(N\) squares be given by its cells in \(\mathbb Z^2\) and its retained joins, as in Lemma B1(a). Whether \(P\) is simply connected can be decided in \(O(N)\) time. If it is, the problem CubeFolding of [ALL25, Section 4.1], whether \(P\) folds onto the cube, can be decided in \(O(N^7)\) time.

Times are for a unit-cost random-access machine. The squares are numbered, and each retained join is given by the numbers of its two squares, so that, once the joins have been read, the square joined to a given square across a given side, if there is one, is found in constant time. The input has size \(O(N)\), since \(P\) has at most \(2N\) retained joins. A polyomino of [ALL25] given abstractly, by its squares and edge identifications, is brought to this form in \(O(N)\) time by placing its squares along these identifications, as in the proof of Lemma B1(a). Like Theorem A, the second assertion rests on Proposition C3, whose finite claim C2 is checked by computer, and on Theorem L7, Theorem M3, and Corollary D1.

**Proof.** Let \(R\) and \(n\) be the numbers of retained joins and full stars of \(P\); both are counted in \(O(N)\) time from the joins at each square. Since \(P\) is a polyomino, its retained cell-adjacency graph is connected (Lemma B1(a)), so by Lemma B1(b) and the Euler count in its proof, \(P\) is simply connected exactly when \(N-R+n=1\). For an input not known to be connected, connectivity is checked first, also in \(O(N)\) time. Assume from now on that \(P\) is simply connected, so that it is a physical disk.

*Cuts.* A *cut* of \(P\) is a maximal straight run of retained joins along one lattice line whose interior vertices are full stars (Section 4.2; the cutting step of Section 2 applies this notion to any physical disk). Continuing a join straight through full stars and stopping at boundary vertices, as in Part 1 of Lemma L2.2 and in the proof of Lemma R1, shows that every join lies on exactly one cut and that the endpoints of a cut are two distinct boundary vertices. So \(P\) has \(K\le R\le 2N\) cuts, and they are found in \(O(N)\) time. Distinct cuts share no join. A vertex common to two cuts is either a full star, at which both pass straight through and cross, or an endpoint of both. A set of cuts is *noncrossing* when no two of its cuts share a full star.

*Maps from cuts.* Let \(\mathcal S\) be a noncrossing set of cuts, \(r\) a root square, and \(\varphi\) a complete frame of \(r\). We show that exactly one consistent complete-frame map \(\beta_{\mathcal S}\) has root frame \(\varphi\) and has as its 90-degree joins exactly the joins of the cuts in \(\mathcal S\), and that the whole bend arcs of \(\beta_{\mathcal S}\) are the cuts in \(\mathcal S\). Uniqueness is the uniqueness assertion in the proof of Lemma R2: on a connected source, a root complete frame and all join statuses determine at most one map.

For existence, the proof of Lemma R1 from the cutting step on, apart from its last sentence, uses only three properties of the runs: they are straight runs of retained joins whose interior vertices are full stars and whose endpoints are distinct boundary vertices; distinct runs meet at most in common endpoints; and at an interior vertex of a run the two joins off the run lie on no run. The cuts in \(\mathcal S\) have these properties. For the last, the two joins off a cut \(\Gamma\) at an interior vertex \(z\) lie on the cut through \(z\) perpendicular to \(\Gamma\), which crosses \(\Gamma\) and so is not in \(\mathcal S\). Hence removing the joins of the cuts in \(\mathcal S\) leaves \(\left|\mathcal S\right|+1\) physical disk regions, and their adjacency graph \(T_{\mathcal S}\) is a tree with one edge for each cut in \(\mathcal S\), joining the two regions that contain the squares on its two sides; every join between distinct regions lies on a cut in \(\mathcal S\). Removing an edge \(\Gamma'\) from \(T_{\mathcal S}\) leaves two subtrees. The squares of the regions of each subtree form a set that is connected by joins not on \(\Gamma'\), and the only joins between the two sets are those of \(\Gamma'\). So the two sets are the components of the retained cell-adjacency graph without the joins of \(\Gamma'\), that is, the two halves of \(P\) along \(\Gamma'\) in the sense of the cutting step.

Let \(\beta_0\) be the map whose frames are given by the formula of the Colors paragraph of Section 4.2, with root cell \(r\) and root frame \(\varphi\). That formula uses only the positions of the cells: writing \(\varphi(x,y)=o+xu+yw\) and \(r=(x_0,y_0)\), the map \(\beta_0\) is the planar position map followed by \((X,Y)\mapsto o+\tau(X-x_0)\,u+\tau(Y-y_0)\,w\), so it is well defined on \(P\). By the argument given there, \(\beta_0\) is consistent with every retained join of \(P\) a 180-degree join, and it carries all sides on one lattice line onto one complete edge of the face \(F_0\) of \(\varphi\); let \(e(\Gamma)\) be the edge onto which it carries a cut \(\Gamma\).

Root \(T_{\mathcal S}\) at the region \(v_0\) containing \(r\), put \(g_{v_0}=\mathrm{id}\), and for each edge \(\Gamma\) of \(T_{\mathcal S}\) from a region \(p\) to a child \(v\) put \(g_v=g_p\circ\sigma_{e(\Gamma)}\). Define \(\beta_{\mathcal S}\) as \(g_v\circ\beta_0\) on the squares of each region \(v\). Its root frame is \(\varphi\), and region \(v\) maps onto the face \(g_v(F_0)\). Every retained join lies inside one region or on a cut in \(\mathcal S\). At a join inside a region \(v\), both frames of \(\beta_0\) are composed with the same isometry \(g_v\), so the join equations still hold and the join remains a 180-degree join. At a join of a cut \(\Gamma\in\mathcal S\), joining a square of \(p\) to a square of \(v\), the map \(\beta_0\) carries the two identified corners into \(e(\Gamma)\), which \(\sigma_{e(\Gamma)}\) fixes pointwise, so \(g_p\circ\beta_0\) and \(g_v\circ\beta_0=g_p\circ\sigma_{e(\Gamma)}\circ\beta_0\) agree on them and the join equations hold. Since \(\sigma_{e(\Gamma)}\) exchanges \(F_0\) with the other face at \(e(\Gamma)\), the two squares map onto distinct faces, and the join is a 90-degree join. So \(\beta_{\mathcal S}\) is consistent, and its 90-degree joins are exactly the joins of the cuts in \(\mathcal S\). By the continuation rule in the proof of Lemma R1, the run through a 90-degree join of \(\beta_{\mathcal S}\) is the cut containing that join, so the whole bend arcs of \(\beta_{\mathcal S}\) are the cuts in \(\mathcal S\).

*The algorithm.* Fix a root square \(r\) and a complete frame \(\varphi\) of \(r\). For each cut \(\Gamma\), compute \(e(\Gamma)\) and the half of \(P\) along \(\Gamma\) not containing \(r\), that is, the component of the retained cell-adjacency graph without the joins of \(\Gamma\) that misses \(r\); this takes \(O(KN)\) time in all. Record in a \(K\times K\) table which pairs of cuts cross; these are the pairs of cuts through a common full star, exactly one pair at each full star. Record in a second table, for each noncrossing pair \(\Gamma,\Gamma'\), whether \(\Gamma\) lies in the half along \(\Gamma'\) not containing \(r\). This is well defined since, as in the proof of Lemma R1, a cut that does not cross \(\Gamma'\) lies in one half along \(\Gamma'\), apart from common endpoints. For a noncrossing set \(\mathcal S\) and \(\Gamma\in\mathcal S\), let \(v\) be the child region of \(T_{\mathcal S}\) at the edge \(\Gamma\). Removing an edge \(\Gamma'\) from \(T_{\mathcal S}\) leaves two subtrees whose regions form the two halves along \(\Gamma'\). Hence the edges of \(T_{\mathcal S}\) on the path from \(v_0\) to \(v\) are \(\Gamma\) and the cuts \(\Gamma'\in\mathcal S\) for which \(\Gamma\) lies in the half along \(\Gamma'\) not containing \(r\). Of two such edges \(\Gamma',\Gamma''\), the edge \(\Gamma'\) comes first on the path exactly when \(\Gamma''\) lies in the half along \(\Gamma'\) not containing \(r\). So, if \(\Gamma_1,\ldots,\Gamma_m=\Gamma\) are these edges in order from \(v_0\), the recurrence gives \(g_v=\sigma_{e(\Gamma_1)}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{e(\Gamma_m)}\), a product of at most \(\left|\mathcal S\right|\) symmetries read off from the tables; the order matters, since these symmetries need not commute. The regions of \(T_{\mathcal S}\) are \(v_0\) and the child regions of the edges. The algorithm accepts \(P\) if and only if some noncrossing set \(\mathcal S\) with \(5\le\left|\mathcal S\right|\le7\) gives all six faces among \(F_0\) and the colors \(g_v(F_0)\) of these child regions. Each of the \(O(K^7)\) such sets takes \(O(1)\) time, so with \(K\le 2N\) the total time is \(O(N^2+N^7)=O(N^7)\).

*Correctness.* If the algorithm accepts \(P\) with a set \(\mathcal S\), then \(\beta_{\mathcal S}\) is a six-face consistent map with at most seven whole bend arcs, and Theorem L7, Theorem M3, and Corollary D1 give a valid folding of \(P\) onto the cube, as in the proof of Theorem A. Conversely, if \(P\) folds onto the cube, the proof of necessity in Theorem A gives a surjective consistent complete-frame map, and Proposition C3 gives a surjective member \(M\) of its fixed toggle family with at most seven active arcs, which are exactly its whole bend arcs. By Lemma R1 and its proof, these arcs are straight runs of joins through full stars, ending at boundary vertices, and they meet at most in common endpoints. So they are the cuts of a noncrossing set \(\mathcal S\) with \(\left|\mathcal S\right|\le7\), and \(\left|\mathcal S\right|\ge5\) because six colors need six regions. The cube isometries act transitively on complete frames, so \(h\circ M\) has root frame \(\varphi\) for some isometry \(h\). Then \(h\circ M\) is a six-face consistent map with root frame \(\varphi\) whose 90-degree joins are exactly the joins of the cuts in \(\mathcal S\), so \(h\circ M=\beta_{\mathcal S}\) by uniqueness; hence \(\mathcal S\) gives all six faces, and the algorithm accepts \(P\). \(\square\)

Soundness uses Theorem L7, Theorem M3, and Corollary D1, but not Proposition C3; completeness uses Proposition C3. With the analytic bound of Lemma R6 in place of C3, the same enumeration over noncrossing sets of at most twelve cuts decides in \(O(N^{12})\) time whether \(P\) has a surjective consistent complete-frame map, but concluding foldability from such a map requires Theorem A and hence Proposition C3. The algorithm does not search the toggle family of a given map, which can have exponentially many members; the cuts depend only on the source, and Lemma R1 with Proposition C3 confines the search to sets of at most seven of them. We have not analyzed the time needed to construct a folded state.

An implementation of an equivalent form of this procedure was compared with an exhaustive search for surjective consistent maps. For every set of at most seven cuts, the implementation propagates frames from the root along a spanning tree, with the joins of the set at 90 degrees, and checks every join, instead of testing noncrossing and using the tables. The two agreed on every physical disk with at most 11 squares and on every physical disk with all joins retained and at most 13 squares, both up to symmetry, and on 1,140 random physical disks with 11 to 40 squares. None of these needed more than six cuts. Corollary A2 does not depend on this computation, and its scripts are not part of the supplement.

Two further computations, also outside the supplement and used in no proof, indicate how small a disk can be when a change of map is needed and compare the model with independently written code. First, on every physical disk with at most 11 squares, and on every physical disk with all joins retained and at most 12 squares, every surjective consistent map already has locally feasible orders; two separately written programs agree on this where their ranges overlap. The smallest physical disks we found with a surjective consistent map that has no locally feasible orders have 14 squares; we do not know whether 14 is least. We found three, all tree-shaped; they have 2,652, 2,768 and 2,874 surjective consistent maps, counted up to cube isometries, and on each exactly one of these has no locally feasible orders. For comparison, [ALL25, Example 4.5] has 38 squares. Its toggle family has \(2^{21}\) members; the surjective members whose set of active arcs is minimal under inclusion are 432 maps with five active arcs, and all of them have locally feasible orders. Second, we ran a corrected copy, described below, of the implementation of the decision algorithm of [ALL25] that accompanies [LS25], see [Shi25], on all 11,501 simply connected polyominoes with at most 10 squares, or at most 9 squares when there are slits, up to symmetry. Its face-map and layer-map stages find a surjective consistent mapping together with a layer map that passes its self-intersection test exactly when a surjective consistent mapping exists. The test for self-intersecting layer maps, which is the same in the published code, accepts exactly the locally feasible orders of Section 1.2 on 4.7 million pairs of a consistent map and face orders, not reduced by symmetry, on sources with and without holes, on which we compared the two. The corrected copy differs from the published code in three places, which remove two defects. The published code assumes that the first square of the input is the top-left square of the bounding box; for 1,148 of the 11,501 inputs no rotation or reflection brings a square of the polyomino there. And when a vertex labelling gives the top-right corner of the first square two labels, one on each side of a slit, the step that converts the labelling into a face map raises an error and the labelling is dropped; when this happens to every labelling the answer is a wrong negative, as it is for 183 of the other inputs in at least one position. We did not rely on its knot-recognition stage, which stopped with an error on 16 of the inputs and is unnecessary for simply connected polyominoes by [ALL25, Theorem 3.4].

## 6. A finite obstruction to connected monochrome map selection

The following independent observation concerns selection of a consistent map, not nonfoldability. Here a **monochrome component** means a connected component of the retained cell-adjacency graph after deleting every join between different target faces. This is not the literal inverse image of a closed cube face, which can also contain edges and vertices of differently mapped squares. No novelty or minimum-size assertion is made.

**Proposition X1.** The ordinary disk source

\[
P=(\{0,1\}\times\{0,1,2,3\})\cup(\{1,2,3,4\}\times\{3,4\}),
\]

with all geometric joins retained, admits a valid folding onto the cube. It has exactly 81 consistent complete-frame maps after fixing the complete frame of the square \((0,0)\), exactly one of which is surjective. That surjective map has seven monochrome components and twelve retained 90-degree joins. In particular, no surjective consistent map has a single monochrome component on each target face.

**Proof.** First consider a complete \(2\times2\) block. Write a frame as \((n,u,v)\), its outward normal and its source-right/source-up tangent vectors, and let 0 denote a 180-degree join and 1 a 90-degree join. For an east (respectively north) step, from a square to the neighbor across its right (respectively top) side, the neighbor's frame is obtained from the square's frame by \(H_0\) or \(H_1\) (respectively \(V_0\) or \(V_1\)), the subscript being the bit of the join, where

\[
\begin{aligned}
H_0(n,u,v)&=(n,-u,v), &\quad H_1(n,u,v)&=(u,-n,v),\\
V_0(n,u,v)&=(n,u,-v), &\quad V_1(n,u,v)&=(v,u,-n).
\end{aligned}
\]

If \(a,b,c,d\) are the turn bits of the lower east step, the left north step, the upper east step, and the right north step, respectively, and \((n,u,v)\) is the frame of the lower-left square, consistency is exactly

\[
V_dH_a(n,u,v)=H_cV_b(n,u,v).
\]

Substitution in the four displayed formulas gives only

\[
(a,b,c,d)=(0,0,0,0),\ (0,1,0,1),\ (1,0,1,0).
\]

For clarity, when \(a=b=0\), equality of normals forces \(c=d=0\). When \((a,b)=(0,1)\) or \((1,0)\), it forces \((c,d)=(0,1)\) or \((1,0)\), respectively, and the full frames agree. When \(a=b=1\), the two possible equal-normal choices give unequal tangent vectors. This proves the \(2\times2\) assertion without relying on enumerated map files.

Apply it to the three overlapping \(2\times2\) blocks in a \(2\times4\) rectangle. Call a join transverse or longitudinal according as its step is parallel to a short or a long side of the rectangle; the six longitudinal joins form three separators of two parallel joins each. By the uniqueness argument in the proof of Lemma R2, a map is determined by the complete frame of one square and the turn bits of all joins. Conversely, given a frame and bits, propagation by the four displayed formulas and their inverses along the spanning tree formed by the four transverse joins and the three longitudinal joins of one long side assigns a frame to every square. Each of the three remaining joins closes exactly one \(2\times2\) block, so these frames form a consistent map exactly when every block has one of the three patterns above. Hence all four transverse joins have the same turn bit. If that bit is 1, every longitudinal join is 0, giving one map. If the transverse bit is 0, each of the three longitudinal separators independently has bit 0 or 1, with its two parallel joins having that same bit, giving eight maps. Thus a fixed corner frame extends in exactly nine ways.

Call \(\{0,1\}\times\{0,1,2,3\}\) the vertical lobe and \(\{1,2,3,4\}\times\{3,4\}\) the horizontal lobe of \(P\). They meet in the single square \((1,3)\), a corner square of each, and there are no additional joins between their other squares. Fix the complete frame of \((1,3)\). The two choices are independent, giving \(9^2=81\) maps. Fixing any other square's frame, in particular that of \((0,0)\) as in the statement, gives the same count: whole-cube isometries act freely and transitively on the 48 complete frames, and preserve consistency and face coverage.

The exceptional transverse-only map of a lobe covers two faces. The other lobe covers at most four, including the common-square face, so a surjective map must use the longitudinal family on both lobes. Let \(n\) be the common-square normal and \(u,v\) its tangent axes. In the longitudinal family the transverse joins are 180-degree joins. Since \(H_0\), \(V_0\), \(V_1\) and their inverses change the source-right tangent at most in sign, every frame of the vertical lobe has source-right tangent \(\pm u\); since \(V_0\), \(H_0\), \(H_1\) and their inverses change the source-up tangent at most in sign, every frame of the horizontal lobe has source-up tangent \(\pm v\). As a normal is orthogonal to both tangents, the vertical lobe has normals in \(\{\pm n,\pm v\}\), and the horizontal lobe has normals in \(\{\pm n,\pm u\}\). Covering all six faces requires each lobe to attain both signs of its own tangent normal: \(\pm v\) on the vertical lobe and \(\pm u\) on the horizontal lobe.

The common square \((1,3)\) is an end square of both lobes: it lies in the top row of the vertical lobe and in the left column of the horizontal lobe. In the longitudinal family both squares of a row of the vertical lobe, or of a column of the horizontal lobe, have the same normal, since \(H_0\) and \(V_0\) preserve the normal. Starting at the end containing \((1,3)\), three successive 90-degree turns have normal sequence \(n,d,-n,-d\), where \(d\in\{v,-v\}\) on the vertical lobe and \(d\in\{u,-u\}\) on the horizontal lobe. This is the only binary sequence of three turns attaining both \(d\) and \(-d\): with at most one 90-degree turn there is only one non-\(n\) normal; with two, adjacent turns give \(n,d,-n\) with one repetition, whereas the separated pattern 101 returns to \(n\) and never reaches \(-d\). Thus all three longitudinal turns must be 90 degrees in both lobes. This proves uniqueness of the surjective map.

The face opposite the shared-square face occurs in exactly the two separated dominoes

\[
\{(0,1),(1,1)\},\qquad\{(3,3),(3,4)\}.
\]

The common-square face has one connected three-square component, and the other four target faces each have one domino component. There are therefore seven monochrome components. Each lobe contributes three separators of two 90-degree joins, giving twelve such joins altogether.

It remains to prove that the source actually folds. First, \(P\) is homeomorphic to a closed disk; as Section 1.1 requires, this is checked on the quotient, not on the drawing. Place the cell \((i,j)\) on the unit square \([i,i+1]\times[j,j+1]\). All geometric joins are retained, and at every lattice point the squares of \(P\) containing it are consecutive in the four-sector star, so the retained joins identify all their corners there to a single vertex of \(P\). In particular, at the lattice points \((1,4)\) and \((2,3)\), where two squares of \(P\) meet only in a corner, these two squares are joined in this way through the square \((1,3)\). Hence the position map from \(P\) onto the union of its squares in the plane is a continuous bijection of a compact space onto a Hausdorff space, and so a homeomorphism. That union consists of the rectangles \([0,2]\times[0,4]\) and \([1,5]\times[3,5]\), which overlap in a square with nonempty interior; it is a planar disk, since it is star-shaped about an interior point of their overlap and has a simple polygonal boundary. The foldability of this source is not new: it also follows from [ALL25, Theorem 8.2]. By Lemma B1(a) and (b), \(P\) is a simply connected polyomino in the sense of [ALL25], and its bounding box is \([0,5]\times[0,5]\). Orient the boundary of \(P\) and the box clockwise, as in [ALL25, Section 8]. From the boundary vertex \((2,3)\), which does not lie on the box, the boundary of \(P\) first reaches the box at \((5,3)\) in the negative direction and at \((2,0)\) in the positive direction. The nearest corner of the box in the positive direction from \((5,3)\) and the nearest corner in the negative direction from \((2,0)\) are both \((5,0)\), so \((5,0)\) is a valid corner for \((2,3)\). Its horizontal and vertical distances from \((2,3)\) are both \(3\), so the hypothesis of that theorem, distance at least \(3\) in both directions, holds. The explicit witness above keeps the proof self-contained. The assertions about consistent maps and monochrome components do not depend on [ALL25, Theorem 8.2].

The accompanying standalone example package (directory `examples/`) contains the complete witness and a standard-library verifier. It generates all 48 cube frames, reconstructs all 20 retained joins, enumerates the 81 normalized maps under complete endpoint constraints, identifies the unique surjective map, and checks the eleven nonalternating endpoint words and the component and join counts. It runs from outside the research repository with Python isolated mode and site imports disabled. Recorded normal and optimized runs agree, and four corrupted witnesses are rejected in both modes. These checks support transcription and implementation reliability; the proof above does not require a catalog of larger sources or the original repository.

The proposition refutes only the universal requirement of six monochrome components. It provides neither a general bound on their number nor a nonfoldable source, and it does not settle the question whether some surjectively mappable polyomino has no valid folding.
