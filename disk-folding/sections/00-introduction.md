# Square-grid disks with a surjective consistent cube map fold validly onto the cube

Todd S. Sherman

Research manuscript, 5 November 2026; revised 7, 9 and 14 November 2026

*This manuscript and its computational supplement were written and checked by AI agents under the author's direction, using Anthropic's Fable 5.2 and Opus 5.5, Google's Carbon, and OpenAI's Astra. No human with sufficient expertise in this field of study has reviewed any proof.*

### Abstract

We consider folding a physical disk onto the unit cube, allowing multiple layers and 90- or 180-degree folds along retained grid joins. A physical disk is obtained from finitely many unit squares of the plane grid by gluing some pairs of side-adjacent squares along their common side, and is homeomorphic to a closed disk. We prove that a consistent complete-frame map covering all six cube faces is sufficient for a valid folding in the piecewise-linear ambient-isotopy model. The source, including its slits and corner identifications, is unchanged; the map and layer orders may change. The proof decomposes a map into monochrome disks separated by whole straight crease arcs, constructs a family of maps indexed by arc toggles, and bounds a minimal six-covering member by twelve arcs. An exhaustive certificate on 87,538 abstract colored trees reduces the bound to seven. A geometric ordering argument then realizes the resulting map, and disk topology gives validity. The theorem has no area, interface-length, or retained-dual-rank bound. The proof, including the certificate, also yields an \(O(N^7)\)-time algorithm deciding whether a physical disk with \(N\) squares folds onto the cube, since the candidate crease arcs depend only on the source and at most seven are needed. Both results concern disk sources; they do not settle the corresponding existence and complexity questions for sources with holes.

## Introduction

A face assignment can satisfy every square-edge incidence and still fail to describe a realizable layered folding. Conversely, an unrealizable prescribed map does not show that its source cannot fold: another map may admit compatible layers. The distinction between a particular map and the existence of some valid map is central here.

**Theorem A.** Let \(P\) be a physical disk, that is, a physical source in the sense of Section 1.1 that is homeomorphic to a closed disk, with its retained unit-edge joins, slits, and corner identifications fixed. Then \(P\) admits a valid folding onto the unit cube in the layered PL model if and only if it admits a consistent complete-frame map covering all six cube faces. The sufficient direction introduces no source cuts, gluings, or additional crease lines.

Here a valid folding is, as in Aichholzer, Lehner, and Lindorfer [ALL25, Definition 3.2], a folded state that is ambient isotopic to a trivial folding, that is, to a realization of a flat blueprint. It does not mean a motion in which square panels remain rigid at every intermediate time. Section 1 makes the model precise.

The necessity is immediate. For sufficiency, a 90-degree interface continues straight through each interior vertex and cannot cross another such interface. Whole interfaces cut the disk conceptually into a tree of monochrome disks. Reflecting one side in a symmetry fixing the target cube edge toggles that interface while preserving every retained join. A minimum six-covering member of this finite family has exclusive leaf colors, at most four leaves, and at most twelve active interfaces. Four leaves reduce analytically to five interfaces; the remaining trees are paths or Y-shaped trees. A complete certificate reduces all their possibilities to at most seven interfaces. Section 4 constructs compatible layer orders for these remaining arrangements. Section 1 turns them into an embedded PL disk and hence a valid folding.

The distinction between an interface and a unit join is essential. One interface may contain arbitrarily many joins, and each monochrome region may have arbitrarily many squares. The construction treats the complete sequences of bank ports, not a replacement by single hinges.

### Relation to previous work

Aichholzer et al. [ABDD18] compare folding models and give algorithms for tree-shaped sources, and Czajkowski et al. [CDD+20] study small polyominoes in models with additional creases, such as the half-grid model. Classifications in such models are not assertions in the fixed-grid model considered here. Table 1 of [CDD+20] summarizes the status of cube folding of polyominoes across folding models as of 2020; for the grid model with orthogonal creases and polyominoes whose retained cell-adjacency graphs may contain cycles, it records the problem as partially characterized and open. Czajkowski et al. also report [CDD+20, Section 3] that Dunham and Whieldon [DW18] found by exhaustive search all solutions of Gardner's puzzle, which asks to cut slits into a \(3\times3\) square so that it folds along grid lines into a unit cube with only one side of the paper showing on the outside, and also all solutions without this last condition.

Aichholzer et al. [AAAC21] study polyominoes with holes, use consistent mappings as a necessary test for foldability, and ask [AAAC21, Section 5] whether this necessary condition is also sufficient. Aichholzer, Lehner, and Lindorfer [ALL25] give the PL validity framework used here and exhibit prescribed-map obstructions. In particular, their Example 4.5 is a surjective consistent mapping of a simply connected polyomino that is realized by no folded state, so the sufficient direction of Theorem A may have to change the map. They ask in Question 4.9 whether some polyomino admits surjective pseudo-foldings but no valid one. Lemma B1 in Section 1.6 identifies the polyominoes of [ALL25] (squares identified only along edges) with physical sources, their simply connected polyominoes with physical disks, and their consistent mappings, read as isometric on each square, with consistent complete-frame maps. With this identification, Theorem A shows that no simply connected polyomino admits surjective pseudo-foldings without admitting a valid one (Corollary A1 in Section 5). It does not settle Question 4.9 for polyominoes with holes. Lehner and Shirley [LS25] study cooperating holes in rectangular sources, outside the disk class.

Open Problem 9.2 of [ALL25] asks for a complete characterization of the simply connected polyominoes that can be folded into a cube; there "into" is to be read as "onto", since in the sense of [ALL25, Definition 3.3] every polyomino folds into the cube trivially, by 180-degree folds onto one face [ALL25, Section 4.1, p. 613]. For these polyominoes Theorem A reduces the problem to the existence of a surjective consistent complete-frame map. It gives no characterization in terms of shape. Conjecture 4.10 of [ALL25] states that CubeFolding, the problem of deciding whether a given polyomino folds onto the cube, can be solved in polynomial time. For tree-shaped polyominoes this follows from the classification of [ALL25, Section 7], as Lehner and Shirley note [LS25, Section 1]. Corollary A2 in Section 5 settles the simply connected case: the candidate crease arcs, maximal straight runs of retained joins through full stars, depend only on the source, and by Lemma R1 and Proposition C3 at most seven of them are needed, so CubeFolding for simply connected polyominoes with \(N\) squares can be decided in \(O(N^7)\) time. Like Theorem A, this relies on the finite claim C2 behind Proposition C3, which is checked by computer (Section 3.2). The conjecture remains open for polyominoes with holes.

Two special cases of Theorem A were known. First, tree-shaped polyominoes, those whose retained cell-adjacency graph is a tree, are physical disks, and Aichholzer, Lehner, and Lindorfer [ALL25, Section 7] classify the tree-shaped polyominoes that fold onto the cube. This extends [ABDD18, Theorem 4], which they describe as a characterization, in the grid model, of the foldable tree-shaped polyominoes whose bounding box has height at most 3; they note that its statement and proof appear to be incomplete and give their own formulation and proof. Their arguments for non-foldability show that the trees concerned have no surjective consistent mapping. For \(3\times3\) and \(3\times4\) bounding boxes they rely on computer-generated lists of minimal foldable trees [ALL25, Figures 25–26]. We checked by computer that, up to symmetry, these lists consist exactly of the trees of those bounding sizes that admit a surjective consistent mapping but contain no proper sub-polyomino admitting one. Hence, granted the correctness of these lists, the tree case of Theorem A follows from [ALL25, Section 7]. Second, [ALL25, Theorem 8.2] shows that a simply connected polyomino folds onto the cube if some boundary point has horizontal and vertical distance at least 3 from one of its valid corners, in the sense defined there. This covers many disks that are not trees. It does not imply Theorem A, since some non-tree disks with a surjective consistent map fail its hypothesis; the smallest of these have eight cells. Theorem A covers all physical disks, whose retained cell-adjacency graphs may contain many cycles.

We know of no individual disk whose foldability needs the reduction of Sections 2–4, and none is presented here as a new example. An exhaustive computer search over the non-tree disks that have a surjective consistent map but fail the hypothesis of [ALL25, Theorem 8.2], with at most 12 cells and all joins retained or with at most 10 cells and slits allowed, finds that each of them also folds by more direct means: all but 49 by simple folds in the sense of Arkin et al. [ABD+04], which reduce the disk to a layered cube net that is then folded onto the cube, and the remaining 49 by wrapping a layered tree-shaped stack around the cube, a step whose validity rests on Theorem M3 and Corollary D1. Our computations in this and the preceding paragraph bear only on the relation to previous work; Theorem A does not depend on them, and their scripts are not part of the supplement. The contribution of Theorem A is the statement for all physical disks, not an individual example.

The bank-order lemma, Lemma L2 of Section 4.2, is proved directly, by induction along straight runs of joins. Its topological inputs are the classification of compact PL surfaces and the arc-splitting statement (Sch) of Section 4.2, which follows from the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem [MT01, Section 2.2] and the \(\theta\)-graph lemma [MT01, Section 2.1]. The proof of Lemma R1 in Section 2 uses the same inputs. All geometric reductions are proved below. Section 1 compares its local order condition and its realization theorem with results on flat folded states [ADK26], on folding motions of polygonal paper [DDMO04], and on lifting maps to embeddings [Mel17]. Section 6 gives a supporting example showing why requiring each face's monochrome square subgraph to be connected is too restrictive.

The computational step checks positive witnesses over a proved exhaustive abstract domain. It is separated from the source reduction and geometric realization, so a reviewer can examine the finite claim without trusting the search procedure that found its certificates.
